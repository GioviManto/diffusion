\chapter{Learning the Parameters: EM on the Chain}\label{ch:em-method}

Everything so far assumed the transition kernel was given. This chapter drops
that assumption. The data are still a Markov chain observed through the
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck channel, but the kernel that generated them is unknown and
has to be recovered from the noised sequences alone, no clean data, no
supervision, and no differentiation through the inference.

The tool is expectation--maximisation, and the reason it is cheap here is
Fisher's identity: one complete forward--backward pass of belief propagation
already contains the exact gradient of the marginal log-likelihood with respect
to the parameters. The E-step is the BP recursion of
Chapter~\ref{ch:gaussian-bp} with the kernel held fixed; the M-step moves the
kernel. Nothing new has to be implemented, and nothing has to be
back-propagated through.
\section{Model: an estimated mixture innovation}\label{sec:em-model}

The clean chain is the one of \eqref{eq:l-chain-prior}, in the index
convention of this chapter,
\begin{equation}
  a_1 \sim \Nd(0,1), \qquad a_i = \alpha\, a_{i-1} + \varepsilon_i,
  \qquad i = 2,\dots,n,
  \label{eq:em-chain}
\end{equation}
with innovations $\varepsilon_i$ i.i.d., mean zero, variance
$\ivar = 1-\alpha^2$, the same Gaussian initial state and the same
variance-matching that Chapter~\ref{ch:laplace} used, but now drawn from
a member of a \emph{parametric family} rather than a fixed law. Forward noising is the coordinatewise
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck channel of Section~\ref{sec:ou},
$x = e^{-t} a + \sqrt{\Deltat}\,\xi$, unchanged from every earlier chapter.

The estimated family is a $C$-component Gaussian mixture on the innovation.
It is the transition kernel written $M(a' \mid a)$ in
Chapters~\ref{ch:background} and~\ref{ch:laplace}, carrying its parameters
explicitly because they are now what is being estimated:
\begin{equation}
  K_\theta(a' \mid a) = \sum_{c=1}^{C} \pi_c \,
    \Nd\!\big(a' - \alpha a;\ \nu_c,\ s_c^2\big),
  \qquad \theta = \{\alpha, (\pi_c,\nu_c,s_c)_{c=1}^C\},
  \label{eq:em-mixture-kernel}
\end{equation}
so both the autoregressive coefficient $\alpha$ and the shape of the
innovation density are free parameters, estimated jointly by maximum marginal
likelihood from noised data alone. At $C=1$ this collapses to a single
Gaussian, recovering the model of Chapter~\ref{ch:gaussian}; larger $C$ can
represent skew, heavy tails, and multimodality to the resolution of the
grid. The initial law $a_1 \sim \Nd(0,1)$ is \emph{held fixed} at
estimation time, it is the value used to generate every family compared in
Chapter~\ref{ch:em-results}, so there is no misspecification in it, but
neither is there direct evidence it could itself be recovered from noised
data; that question is not addressed here.

Two assumptions carry the argument and, consistent with the practice of this
thesis, are stated rather than tested by the estimator itself: the clean
process is exactly first-order Markov, and the forward noise is exactly
coordinatewise. Section~\ref{sec:em-nonmarkov} of Chapter~\ref{ch:em-results} measures
what happens when the first of these is false by a controlled amount; the
second is never relaxed anywhere in this thesis.

\begin{remark}[Covariance-stationary, not strictly stationary]
Choosing $\ivar=1-\alpha^2$ makes the chain covariance-stationary --- $\operatorname{Var}(a_i)=1$
and $\operatorname{Cov}(a_i,a_j)=\alpha^{|i-j|}$ hold exactly for \emph{every} innovation
law, by the same second-moment recursion as the Gaussian case. It does not
make the chain strictly stationary: for a non-Gaussian innovation the
invariant marginal law of the chain is not $\Nd(0,1)$, so fixing $a_1\sim
\Nd(0,1)$ starts every family off its own invariant distribution. Nothing
compared in Chapter~\ref{ch:em-results} depends on the distinction, because every
family compared shares the same $a_1$ and the same covariance structure. The
claim available here is covariance-stationarity, and it is the one used.
\end{remark}

\section{What remains exact}\label{sec:em-exact}

Two facts from Section~\ref{sec:l-grid} carry over unchanged, and everything
the estimator does rests on them, so they are worth restating here.

\begin{proposition}[The posterior factor graph is still the prior's chain]
\label{prop:em-unary}
Coordinatewise forward noising contributes exactly one \emph{unary} factor per
site, $x_i \mapsto \phi_i(a_i) \propto \exp\!\big(-(x_i-e^{-t}a_i)^2/2\Deltat\big)$.
It reweights node potentials and creates no new edges among the latent
$a_i$, for any innovation law, mixture or otherwise. The posterior over the
clean chain given the noised observation therefore factorises on exactly the
same chain graph as the prior.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[The E-step is exact]\label{prop:em-estep}
Since the posterior graph is a tree, one forward and one backward
sum--product sweep return the exact single-site and pairwise marginals of the
posterior, at the level of functional messages, for \emph{any} innovation
law. Consequently the E-step of an EM procedure for $\theta$ requires no
variational approximation and no sampling: the sufficient statistics EM needs
are exact conditional expectations under the true posterior, mixture label
included.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof.} Both are consequences of the chain being a tree
(Theorem~\ref{thm:tree-exact}): the observation factors are unary, so they
reweight nodes without joining any pair, and sum--product on a tree returns
exact marginals whatever the factors are. Neither step assumes the innovation
law admits a closed-form message update. \hfill$\square$

 What is \emph{not} exact is the representation of a functional
message on a computer, which is where Section~\ref{sec:em-numerical} and the
grid-BP machinery of Section~\ref{sec:l-grid} enter.

\section{The EM algorithm}\label{sec:em-algorithm}

The latent variables are the clean states $a$ \emph{and} the mixture labels
$c = (c_i)$, so write the complete-data kernel with its label exposed,
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde K_\theta(a', c \mid a)
  \;=\; \pi_c\, \Nd\!\big(a' - \alpha a;\ \nu_c,\ s_c^2\big),
  \qquad
  K_\theta(a' \mid a) = \sum_{c} \widetilde K_\theta(a', c \mid a).
  \label{eq:em-complete-kernel}
\end{equation}
Fisher's identity then gives the gradient of the \emph{marginal}
log-likelihood $\mathcal L(\theta)$ as a posterior expectation of the
complete-data score,
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\theta \mathcal L(\theta)
  \;=\; \E_{a, c \,\mid\, x, \theta}
        \left[\; \sum_{i=2}^{n}
        \nabla_\theta \log \widetilde K_\theta(a_i, c_i \mid a_{i-1}) \right]
  \;=\; \big\langle \Xi(\theta),\ \nabla_\theta \log \widetilde K_\theta \big\rangle ,
  \label{eq:em-fisher}
\end{equation}
where $\Xi(\theta)$ collects the exact posterior sufficient statistics BP
supplies at the current parameter --- the conditional expectations of the
pairwise sums, the label responsibilities, and the second moments that
\eqref{eq:em-complete-kernel} requires --- and the pairing is the sum over
edges of the corresponding expectations. The initial law and the observation
channel carry no $\theta$, so neither contributes a term.
The practical content of \eqref{eq:em-fisher} is that belief propagation is
never differentiated through: BP supplies the conditional expectation
$\Xi(\theta)$ at the current parameter, and the gradient with respect to
$\theta$ is then an ordinary derivative of the kernel's log-density. This
is what makes the E-step of Proposition~\ref{prop:em-estep} directly usable
inside an optimiser.

The M-step for the mixture kernel is \emph{not} an exact maximisation. Given
$\Xi(\theta^{(k)})$, updating $(\pi_c,\nu_c,s_c)_{c=1}^C$ exactly would
require solving a mixture-likelihood problem with its own latent label, so
the implementation alternates closed-form \emph{conditional} maximisers, the mixture block and $\alpha$, until the per-edge gain in $Q$ falls below
a tolerance or a cap of sixteen sweeps is reached, recording on the returned
kernel which of the two occurred. The algorithm is therefore generalised EM,
or ECM \citep{meng1993maximum}, not exact EM: a sweep ascends $Q$
without maximising it.

The cap of sixteen is set by measurement, not convention. Varying only this
budget at $C=8$, $n=256$, and a fixed outer count of $40$, the fitted
innovation excess kurtosis moves by $28\%$ between four sweeps and sixteen
($1.68 \to 2.14$, against a true value of $3.0$) while $\alpha$ moves in the
fourth decimal, the same rate asymmetry between shape and correlation that
Section~\ref{sec:em-convergence} finds in the outer loop, appearing one level
down. The effect is confined to short outer runs: by $60$ outer iterations
the two loops substitute for one another and the inner budget stops
mattering. Sixteen removes the interaction rather than requiring a reader to
establish which regime a given cell is in.

Monotone ascent of $\mathcal L$ is guaranteed by the standard EM argument
applied to the surrogate $Q(\theta \mid \theta^{(k)}) = \langle
\Xi(\theta^{(k)}), \log \widetilde K_\theta\rangle$, ascending $Q$ is all
monotonicity requires, and is checked directly: across an executable audit
of the implementation, the minimum increase in $Q$ over twelve consecutive
steps was $+2.61$ for a Gaussian kernel, $+4.34$ for Laplace, and $+4.18$
for an eight-component mixture, with zero decreases recorded in any logged
run. The returned kernel's evidence equals
\texttt{trace.log\_evidence[-1]} by construction, with a regression test
asserting it, so the reported convergence trace always describes the model
actually returned.

\section{The numerical representation: grid BP, again}\label{sec:em-numerical}

Messages remain functions on $\R$, so they are represented exactly as in
Section~\ref{sec:l-grid}: values on $\ngrid$ equispaced points in $[-A,A]$, updates
by trapezoidal quadrature. Two error sources are tracked separately here as
well. Truncation error, the probability mass the finite domain $[-A,A]$
misses, is diagnosed by the maximum boundary mass over the sites of a chain.
That is a statistic of a sampled trajectory rather than a bound, so it is
recorded over $256$ chains per configuration: the worst chain falls from
$2.3\times10^{-3}$ at $A=4$ to $1.2\times10^{-6}$ at $A=8$, the $90$th
percentile from $2.7\times10^{-4}$ to $1.5\times10^{-8}$, and neither depends on
the grid resolution $\ngrid$. The gap between the two rows matters, because a figure
read off a single trajectory sits near the median and so understates the tail by
two orders of magnitude. Quadrature error, from the
finite spacing $h=2A/(\ngrid-1)$, the grid holds $\ngrid$ points including both
endpoints, on an otherwise exact interior update, falls as
$O(h^2)$: a factor of four per halving of $h$, matching the trapezoidal
rate on a smooth integrand. At the working configuration used for every
experiment in this chapter and in Chapters~\ref{ch:em-kernel}
and~\ref{ch:em-results}, $\ngrid=401$, $A=8$, both sources sit far
below any effect subsequently measured: the discretised recursion reproduces
the closed-form Gaussian posterior mean to $9.2\times10^{-15}$, and agrees
with an independently coded brute-force enumeration, sharing no code with
the BP recursion, to $1.6\times10^{-14}$ on posterior means and
$1.8\times10^{-15}$ on the log-evidence.

\paragraph{Which finite-grid model is being optimised.} ``Exact for the
discretised chain'' needs one more specification than it has been given so far,
because there are two conventions available and they lead to different
objectives. The transition array used here is the continuum density
$K_\theta(a' \mid a)$ evaluated at the grid points and combined with the
trapezoidal weights, with \emph{no} per-column renormalisation. BP is therefore
exact, up to floating point, for the chain-shaped \emph{factor model} that
array defines, and that model is not literally a normalised finite-state
transition kernel on the truncated domain. The distance between the two is
precisely the column-mass residual already reported as the quadrature
diagnostic. At the working configuration, the worst column with $|a| \le A/2$
departs from unit mass by $\gdinterior$; such a column sits far enough inside
the domain that its transition density has no tail outside the grid, so its
residual measures quadrature alone. The worst column anywhere departs by
$\gdedge$, at the domain edge where the density is truncated.

The alternative convention, renormalising each parent-conditioned column
under the quadrature weights, would make the array a genuine finite-state
kernel. Its normaliser would then depend on $\theta$, most strongly near the
boundary, and that dependence would have to be carried: the derivative of
$\log Z_\theta$ would enter both Fisher's identity and the M-step, and the
surrogate could no longer be written as
$Q(\theta \mid \theta^{(k)}) = \langle \Xi(\theta^{(k)}), \log \widetilde K_\theta\rangle$
with $K_\theta$ the raw density. Because no renormalisation is applied, that
term is absent by construction and not by oversight, and the surrogate above
is the correct one for the model actually fitted. The price is that the fitted
object is the continuum kernel read through a quadrature rule, which is what the
column-mass residual measures and what the resolution gate of
Section~\ref{sec:em-numerical} bounds.

above keep apart: resolution and truncation are refined one at a time, so that
neither diagnostic can be mistaken for a certificate of the other.

\begin{remark}[The boundary-mass diagnostic is empirical, not a bound]
\label{rmk:em-boundary}
A boundary-mass figure computed from one sampled chain does not bound the
truncation error for every run, and the gap is large enough to matter. Running
the same recursion on one chain and on $\gdchains$ of them, at the working
configuration and over all $\gdlevels$ noise levels, the batch maximum exceeds
the single-chain reading by a median factor of $\gdboundmed$ and by as much as
$\gdboundhi$; at the mildest level it is only $\gdboundlo$. A single draw
therefore lands somewhere in the body of a distribution with a very long tail,
and where it lands is not knowable from the draw. This is why the figures above
are reported as a maximum and a $90$th percentile over chains rather than as one
number. The diagnostic is an empirical estimate of typical truncation
loss, not a certified bound. What it does \emph{not} affect is the separation of truncation from
quadrature error reported above, which is a pure function of $(A,\ngrid)$ and the
prior and involves no sampled chain at all, that comparison reproduces
to $0.000$ relative difference under reseeding and is exactly the result
Section~\ref{sec:l-grid} already relies on.
\end{remark}

One further identity is worth restating from Section~\ref{sec:l-grid} because
it governs every result in Chapter~\ref{ch:em-results} exactly as it governed
Chapter~\ref{ch:laplace}: whatever an approximation, Gaussian closure, grid
truncation, or an under-converged fit, does to the posterior mean is
amplified by $e^{-t}/\Deltat \sim 1/2t$ in the score, so small diffusion times
remain the regime where any deficiency is most visible.

the third is what makes it an estimator.

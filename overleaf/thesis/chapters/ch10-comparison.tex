\chapter{What Structure Buys, and Where It Stops Paying}
\label{ch:em-results}

Chapter~\ref{ch:em-method} built an estimator that is exact in its inference
step and approximate only in its numerical representation and its
optimisation budget. This chapter asks what that estimator is worth. Five
questions organise it, in the order the evidence answers them most cleanly.

\begin{quote}
How much does the structured estimator reduce denoising error at a given
amount of data; how much of that reduction survives against a baseline that is
given the locality but not the model; how much mixture capacity does the
estimator need; does the pointwise advantage survive when the estimated score
is used to \emph{generate}, by integrating the reverse process; and how far can
the Markov assumption itself be wrong before the structure stops helping?
\end{quote}

\noindent Three are answered outright. The capacity question is answered for
the estimator as implemented, and in the opposite direction to the one a
smaller design suggested, but not for mixture capacity in the abstract, and
Section~\ref{sec:em-capacity} says exactly where that line falls. The
generative-fidelity question is not answered at all, and the section that
addresses it says why the experiment as run cannot settle it. The summary at the
end of the chapter states each of the five at the strength its evidence
supports.

Every comparison below fits the mixture kernel of \eqref{eq:em-mixture-kernel}
by the EM procedure of Chapter~\ref{ch:em-method} and compares it against
neural denoisers trained by ordinary denoising score matching on the same
noised data, with no access to the Markov factorisation, the linear kernel
form, or homogeneity across sites --- three structural facts the estimator is
given and the networks are not. Two network architectures recur throughout: a
fully connected MLP with no locality bias, and a weight-shared local network
with a fixed receptive field. The second is a \emph{locality-respecting}
baseline, not one matched to the estimator: EM--BP smooths over the whole chain
and has no radius of its own to match. The comparison measures the value of the
structure supplied, not a general claim about neural denoisers.

\section{Pointwise data efficiency}\label{sec:em-pointwise}

\subsection{What is being measured}\label{sec:em-estimand}

Every number in this chapter is a ratio of two relative $L^2$ score errors, and
because the ratio is what the conclusions rest on, the definition is written
out here rather than deferred.

Fix a diffusion time $t$ and a test set $\{x^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{J}$ of noised
sequences drawn from the model of Section~\ref{sec:em-model}. Let
$\score^{\star}(x,t) = \nabla_x \log p_t(x)$ be the exact joint score, available
in closed form for the Gaussian chain and from the validated grid reference of
Section~\ref{sec:l-grid} otherwise. For an estimator $\widehat{\score}$, the
error on that test set is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E}\big[\widehat{\score}\big]
  \;=\;
  \frac{\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\big\lVert \widehat{\score}(x^{(j)},t) - \score^{\star}(x^{(j)},t)\big\rVert_2^2\Big)^{1/2}}
       {\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\big\lVert \score^{\star}(x^{(j)},t)\big\rVert_2^2\Big)^{1/2}},
  \label{eq:em-relerr}
\end{equation}
the ratio of two Euclidean norms over the whole test set, so that a value of
$0.1$ means the estimate is wrong by a tenth of the signal's own size. This is
\codefile{metrics.rel\_l2} applied to the stacked residual, and it is scale-free
by construction: multiplying the data by a constant leaves it unchanged.

The two arms are the EM--BP estimator of Chapter~\ref{ch:em-method}, which is
given the Markov factorisation and estimates $\ivar$ and $\corr$ from the same
$\nseq$ noised sequences the network sees, and a denoising-score-matching
network trained on those sequences by \eqref{eq:sm-dsm} and given no structural
information at all. Writing $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{net}}$ and
$\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{EM}}$ for their errors on a common test set, the reported
quantity is
\begin{equation}
  R \;=\; \frac{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{net}}}{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{EM}}},
  \qquad R > 1 \ \text{means the structured estimator is more accurate,}
  \label{eq:em-ratio}
\end{equation}
evaluated cell by cell, where a cell is one (training-set size, seed) pair.
Both arms are re-fitted independently in every cell.

Two aggregation choices remain, and they matter because $R$ is a ratio of
means and not a mean of ratios. This chapter reports the seed-first estimand:
errors are averaged over seeds at fixed size, and the ratio is formed from
those averages. Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation} recomputes the same frozen cells
under four alternatives; the direction and the order of magnitude are the same
in all of them, and the estimand quoted here is systematically the largest,
which is stated wherever the number appears.

\subsection{The measured advantage}

Under the structural assumptions of Section~\ref{sec:em-model}, at the data
budgets tested, the
structured estimator attains lower relative denoising error than the global
MLP by a factor of $\ratiolo$--$\ratiohi$ across the \nsizesused{} training-set
sizes (Table~\ref{tab:pointwise}, Figure~\ref{fig:em-sample-eff}). The factor
is \emph{not} monotone in the budget, and it is the largest of several
defensible aggregations of the same cells --- Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation}
gives the other three, with a paired interval on the one quoted here. These figures are generated by
\codefile{tools/make\_tab\_efficiency.py} from the frozen outputs rather than
transcribed, so the table, the figure and this sentence cannot drift apart.

That first comparison is against a network with neither a locality prior nor
weight sharing. When those two biases are supplied through the window-head
baseline, the observed ratio narrows to
$\structratiolo$--$\structratiohi\times$, measured at the same headline protocol
and reported with its own scoping in Section~\ref{sec:em-architecture}. This
change does not identify a causal decomposition: architecture, parameter
sharing, capacity and optimisation change together across the two baselines. It
shows that the generic-MLP comparison overstates what can be attributed
specifically to the supplied Markov model. The relevant locality result is the
influence decay of Chapter~\ref{ch:gaussian}.

Every tunable quantity either arm is allowed to choose is chosen on a
validation bundle disjoint from both the training set and the test set, and the
two arms are given the same privilege: the network selects its output
parameterisation, predicting the noise or the clean signal, \emph{and} its
training length, EM--BP its iteration count. That choice coincides with the
test-set optimum in $\netagree\%$ of cells for the network and $\emagree\%$ for
EM--BP. A dedicated audit of the selection rule, over $\valselcells$ cells at
$\nseedsused$ seeds, puts a size on what the remainder costs: the mean advantage
is $\valselratiosel\times$ under validation selection and
$\valselratioorc\times$ when each arm is instead given its test-set optimum, a
difference far smaller than the spread across seeds. The protocol is the
defensible one independently of the size of the correction it produces.

\section{How much of the margin is the architecture?}\label{sec:em-architecture}

A ratio against a fully connected network cannot separate knowing the Markov
structure from sharing weights across sites, and the second is the cheaper
explanation. This section measures against a structure-aware baseline at the
headline protocol: the same twelve noise levels and the same sixteen seeds as
Table~\ref{tab:pointwise}, every site scored rather than the centre alone, the
baseline's architecture and radius fixed by a screen on separate development
seeds, and each arm's checkpoint chosen on a validation bundle disjoint from
test. The deployment and provenance conditions are in
Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

\paragraph{Which architecture, and under what search.} Three candidates were
screened on $\screendevseeds$ development seeds, disjoint from the
$\structseeds$ used below: a weight-shared window head (which cannot propagate
past its radius however much data it sees), a dilated convolutional stack, and a
bidirectional message-passing network (both able, in principle, to propagate
across the whole chain). Each was run over its own hyperparameter grid, a
shared learning-rate grid, both output parameterisations, and a common
checkpoint ladder from $\screenckptlo$ to $\screenckpthi$ steps, $\screenconfigs$ configurations in all, scored on the \screenregion{} region.
\textbf{The window head won}, at radius $\screenwinnerradius$ and width
$\screenwinnerwidth$, with the \screenrunnerup{} network next
($\screenwinnerrisk$ against $\screenrunneruprisk$).

A ratio against a fully connected network is not comparable to this one: a
large fraction of that headline margin disappears once locality and weight
sharing are supplied. What survives against a baseline that has them, the
window head, is $\structratiolo$--$\structratiohi\times$, all-site scored,
across the same four sizes as Table~\ref{tab:pointwise}. EM--BP is more
accurate in every one of the $\structcells$ scored cells; that count is
$\structseeds$ seeds $\times$ $\structsizes$ sizes $\times$ $\structregions$
scoring regions (\structregionlist{}), of which the table displays two, and it
is a descriptive diagnostic rather than a replication count, the regions and
sizes share fitted models and training data, so the inferential unit behind each
displayed row is the $\structseeds$ paired seeds.

The ratio increases across the four sizes tested, from
$\structratiolo\times$ to $\structratiohi\times$. That is a description of four
measurements and nothing more is claimed from it. In particular it is not
evidence that the residual is non-architectural. An architecture with an
irreducible approximation floor $b^2$ would give
$R_{\text{window}}/R_{\text{EM}} = (b^2 + c_w/N)/(c_e/N)$, which grows linearly
in $N$ for an entirely architectural reason. The raw risks in
Table~\ref{tab:structured} are consistent with exactly that: EM--BP's risk
falls by a factor of about four and a half across the range, while the window
head's falls by less than two. Different statistical rates or different
optimisation limits would produce the same signature. The direction is
measured; the mechanism is not.

Both arms choose their budget symmetrically, on a validation bundle disjoint
from test, \emph{within the budget grids on offer}: $\structcapem\%$ of EM--BP
cells and $\structcapwindow\%$ of window-head cells select the last rung of
their respective ladder. Selecting the last rung is not proof of convergence, it is a sign that validation error was still falling where the grid ran out, and
that a wider grid might have improved that arm further. So the reported values
compare the two procedures at stated, validation-selected computational budgets,
not at converged optima. Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation} reports the
same ratio under $\structaggcount$ aggregations of the same cells; none of them,
at any size, falls below $\structaggfloor\times$.

\section{How much mixture capacity is worth having}\label{sec:em-capacity}

Enlarging $C$ in \eqref{eq:em-mixture-kernel} should help until the mixture can
represent the true innovation law and stop helping after. The design that tests
this pairs each capacity against $C=1$ --- a single Gaussian innovation, the
kernel the estimator would use if it did not model the innovation law at all ---
on identical training and evaluation data within each cell, at $\capseeds$
seeds and two training sizes, with three initialisations per cell and the
reported one selected on validation. The fits are run to their
plateau and not to a fixed iteration count, because
Section~\ref{sec:em-convergence} measures the fitted shape as settling far later
than the correlation coefficient does, and shape is what capacity moves.

\noindent The result is that \textbf{under this procedure --- this grid, this
generalised M-step, this validation rule and this iteration cap --- no mixture
capacity improves on a single Gaussian innovation} at either training size. The
qualification is quantitative. Every cell at $C \geq \capcappedfrom$ stops at
the cap rather than at the tolerance, and the share of fits the grid cannot
resolve grows with $C$, so the design cannot separate a statistical
disadvantage of larger mixtures from an optimisation or a discretisation one.
What it can say, it says at every seed. The means are dominated by a few
large-magnitude seeds, so the sign count carries the claim: at $C=8$ the mixture
is ahead on only $\capcbestlo$ to $\capcbestwins$ of $\capseeds$ paired seeds,
with median paired differences of $\capmedhi$ and $\capmedlo$ per edge. The
same holds on schedule-level score risk.

The predeclared equivalence test sharpens this rather than softening it. The
region --- $|\Delta(\text{evidence/edge})| < 10^{-4}$ together with
$|\Delta R|/R < 1\%$ --- was fixed before the contrast was computed, precisely so
that ``$C=8$ and $C=16$ are equivalent'' could be asserted or refused rather
than inferred from a failure to reject. It is refused. At
$\nseq = \capheadsize$ the $C=8 \to C=16$ step is \capheadverdict{} against the
region and in the direction of $C=16$ being \emph{worse}: evidence
$\capheadlogev$ with interval $\capheadlogevci$, risk $\capheadrisk$ with
interval $\capheadriskci$, both lying wholly outside it. At
$\nseq = \capothersize$ it is \capotherverdict. So neither ``capacity saturates
by eight'' nor ``capacity keeps paying past eight'' is supported. What is
measured is narrower: at $\nseq = \capheadsize$, the observed $C=8 \to C=16$
contrast is resolved outside the predeclared equivalence region, in the
direction of worse held-out evidence and worse score risk for $C=16$.

\begin{remark}[Two things this does not isolate]\label{rmk:em-capacity-caveats}
Both point the same way, and both are reasons to read the table as a statement
about this estimator on this grid and not about mixture capacity as such.
First, every cell at $C \geq \capcappedfrom$ stopped at the
$\capiterlimit$-iteration cap rather than at the convergence tolerance
($\capcappedcells$ of $\capcappedtotal$). The cap was placed at the measured
practical plateau. A representative $C=16$ fit taken to $2000$ iterations has
per-edge gain $6.9\times10^{-8}$ at $400$, about $6\times10^{-9}$ by $800$, and
then does not move: $6.08\times10^{-9}$ at $1200$ against
$6.09\times10^{-9}$ at $2000$, never reaching the strict tolerance at any
practical count. The residual asymmetry is therefore small, but it is not zero,
and it disfavours the higher capacities. Second, the share of fits whose narrowest component falls
below the grid's resolution floor of $\capsminfloor$ spacings climbs with
capacity, from $\capunresolvedone/\capunresolvedtotal$ at $C=1$ to
$\capunresolvedhi/\capunresolvedtotal$ at $C=16$. Part of what the larger
mixtures are fitting is therefore narrower than the grid can represent, which is
a property of the discretisation and not of the innovation law. Separating the
two would need a finer grid at fixed capacity, which is not run here.
\end{remark}

\noindent A smaller design --- six seeds, one training size, one initialisation
and a fixed $\convbudget$-iteration budget --- reported the opposite sign, and
the reversal is worth recording rather than quietly dropping: it does not
reproduce at sixteen seeds and a plateau-stopped budget. Which of the two
readings the budget produced, and by what mechanism, is not separately tested
here; \compendium{} carries the comparison.

\begin{remark}[Why the iteration budget, and not the capacity, has to be
controlled]\label{rmk:em-capacity-confound}
The reason the design above runs to a plateau is that a fixed budget cannot
distinguish the two. Section~\ref{sec:em-convergence} measures the innovation
shape as settling at a median of $\convshapemed$ updates against
$\convrhomed$ for the correlation coefficient --- a factor of $\convratio$
formed within each fit and then aggregated, with a largest observed value of
$\convshapemax$. Against a fixed $\convbudget$-iteration budget,
$\convunsettled$ of the $\convunsettledof$ configurations in that sweep had not
settled in the shape coordinate. Because a larger $C$ has more shape
parameters to move within the same budget, under-iteration alone produces a
curve that looks exactly like a capacity effect whether or not capacity is doing
anything. This is why
any quantity resting on fitted or generated \emph{shape} at a fixed budget, the
withdrawn reverse-dynamics comparison included, is evidence about an
under-converged operating point and not about the converged estimator.
\end{remark}

Per-iteration runtime changes little over the tested capacity range,
$13.5\to14.3$ seconds from $C=2$ to $C=16$ while the number of free parameters
grows $6\to48$, because the E-step's $O(\nsites\,\ngrid^2)$ cost dominates the
component-dependent M-step. This shows that increasing $C$ adds little cost to
one iteration. It does not remove the confounds above: the higher-capacity fits
are precisely the ones for which the iteration-cap and grid-resolution
diagnostics become less favourable.


\section{Where the Markov assumption stops paying}\label{sec:em-nonmarkov}

Every result so far assumes the clean chain is exactly first-order Markov.
Two controlled violations were measured against an exact reference (linear
Gaussian algebra for the contaminated model, so the estimator being wrong is
not confused with the reference being wrong): a rank-one global latent
shared across all sites at strength $\beta$, and a long-range coupling at
strength $\gamma$ added on top of the chain. This experiment has its own noise
schedule and its own training size, so its ratios measure how the advantage
responds to misspecification and are not on the same scale as
Table~\ref{tab:pointwise}'s; only the direction and the crossing point are read
from them. Both arms train at the headline protocol's budget.

The measurement is reported for two innovation laws. The Gaussian arm carries
both mechanisms, since its contaminated model has an exact reference in closed
form; the Laplace arm carries the rank-one mechanism, where the same
contamination can be applied to a non-Gaussian chain and the reference computed
on the grid. Reading them together separates what is a property of the chain
structure from what is a property of Gaussianity. On the rank-one mechanism the
two agree in shape, the same ordering, the same smooth decay, no crossing, and differ at the margin. At $\beta = \nmbetamax$ the Gaussian arm is still
ahead at every noise level against both baselines, worst ratio
$\nmbetaminratio$; the Laplace arm is still ahead against the convolutional
baseline, worst ratio $\nmlapbetaminratiocnn$, but reaches the break-even line
against the fully connected one at the largest diffusion time, at
$\nmlapbetaminratio$. The ordering carries across both innovation laws tested;
the size of the margin it leaves does not quite. Whether it carries beyond these
two families is not established here.

\noindent The two violations behave qualitatively differently, and the
difference is principled rather than incidental. A global latent shared across
every site makes the score residual \emph{exactly} rank one, by a Woodbury
identity, and over the range tested a chain-structured estimator accommodates
that correction within its own fitted coefficients: the fitted correlation
climbs from $\nmrhoclean$ on the clean chain to $\nmrhobetamax$ at
$\beta = \nmbetamax$: the fitted chain partially accommodates the rank-one
perturbation by changing its effective lag-one correlation. This is partial
accommodation over a measured range, not an exact representation, a first-order Markov family does not contain the contaminated
law, but the residual it cannot represent stays small enough that the
advantage never inverts: at $\beta = \nmbetamax$, where half the marginal
variance is a shared global constant, the ratio at its worst noise level is
still $\nmbetaminratio$ against the weaker of the two baselines.

Long-range coupling is only \emph{approximately} low rank, and a chain family
cannot represent it at all. The advantage against the convolutional baseline is
gone by $\gamma = \nmcrossover$, having survived $\gamma = \nmlastholding$, and
by $\gamma = 0.2$ the estimator loses to both baselines and not to one; at
$\gamma = \nmgammamax$ the ratio at its worst noise level is
$\nmgammaminratio$, where Table~\ref{tab:nonmarkov} reports the larger
schedule-averaged value, and its own relative score error reaches
$\nmemworsterr\%$.

Two rows of the table separate the mechanism from the outcome. The estimator's
own error is what moves in both blocks, the baselines barely change, so
neither column is a story about the networks improving. The last row of each block is what
distinguishes the two violations. Under rank-one contamination the fitted chain
stays within $\nmcldevbeta$ of the Chow--Liu correlation, the best first-order
approximation of the contaminated truth, so its fitted lag-one correlation
tracks the corresponding first-order projection. That is one scalar agreeing,
not the whole fitted law certified. Under long-range coupling the same gap is
$\nmcldevfactor$ times larger, which is what it looks like when the family can
no longer represent what generated the data. The uncontaminated controls of the two blocks are the
same configuration reached from two directions, and they agree to within $2\%$
($\nmcontrolbeta$ and $\nmcontrolgamma$), which is what makes the rest of the
table readable across blocks.

\begin{remark}
This extends the lesson of Chapter~\ref{ch:laplace}: there, what broke
under a harder innovation law was the \emph{representability of messages} in
a finite-dimensional family, while the tree, the two-sweep schedule, and the
score--posterior identity survived unconditionally. Here, the further
question is what breaks when the graph is not a tree to begin with. The
answer is not "misspecification degrades the advantage" uniformly, it is
that a chain-structured estimator tolerates the one violation it can
absorb into its own parameters and fails on the one it structurally cannot
represent, and the two are not interchangeable amounts of "how wrong the
model is": they are different in kind.
\end{remark}

\section{The channel removes shape information faster than correlation information}
\label{sec:em-information}

Section~\ref{sec:em-convergence} found that the innovation shape converges
far slower than the autoregressive coefficient under EM. A direct
information-theoretic measurement runs alongside that finding.

This calculation complements the convergence experiment but does not derive its
iteration count. The table below measures how much statistical information
about each coordinate survives the observation channel. Local EM convergence is
governed instead by the fraction of missing information, equivalently by the
Jacobian of the EM update near its fixed point. The two measurements point in
the same direction here, but they answer different questions, and a flatter
observed likelihood does not by itself entail a given number of updates.

\paragraph{What is computed.} The innovation law is a generalised Gaussian
$f_p(\varepsilon) \propto \exp(-|\varepsilon/s|^{p})$, with $s$ fixed by
$\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = \ivar = 1-\alpha^2$ at every $p$, so
$p$ moves the fourth moment at \emph{exactly fixed} variance and is a
genuine shape coordinate rather than a reparameterised scale. For a
three-parameter vector $\theta = (\alpha, \ivar, p)$, Fisher's identity
\eqref{eq:em-fisher} supplies the exact per-chain score of the marginal
log-likelihood from one forward--backward pass, and the information matrix
is estimated in \emph{outer-product} form, $J = D^{-1}\sum_d g_d g_d^\T$
over $D=512$ chains of $\nsites=32$ sites, at $\ngrid=401$, $A=8$. This is the
outer-product estimator, not the observed negative Hessian, and it is an
estimate at the true parameter rather than at a fitted one. The reported
quantity is the \emph{efficient} information
$I_{p\cdot(\alpha,\ivar)} = 1/(J^{-1})_{pp}$, which is what remains
about $p$ once the nuisance coordinates must be estimated too --- equal
to the diagonal entry only when the coordinates are orthogonal and strictly
smaller otherwise. The gap between $J_{pp}$ and this quantity is
recorded per cell as the nuisance cost, and the condition number of $J$ is
recorded alongside it, since the inversion is where a near-singular $J$
would show up.

\noindent This is a property of the noised likelihood itself, independent of
any optimiser: at any fixed sample size, and at every diffusion time past the
smallest ones, there is less information about the shape coordinate in noised
data than about the correlation. That is consistent with the shape coordinate
being the slow one, though as noted above it does not derive the rate. It is also the reason
the capacity sweep of Section~\ref{sec:em-capacity} is exactly the kind of
measurement where an insufficient iteration budget is dangerous: the
coordinate that is hardest to move is also the one with the least signal per
observation.

Two limits on how far these figures should be pushed. They are ratios of
outer-product estimates at $D=512$ chains, so only the order is meaningful
and the entries are printed to two significant digits for that reason. And the
comparison is across the tested noise grid rather than a limit: the decay
is measured between $t=0.05$ and $t=1.6$, and how it continues past the
schedule's end is not established here. What the table supports is the
direction and the order, shape information collapses far faster than
correlation information, which is the claim the rest of the chapter
rests on.

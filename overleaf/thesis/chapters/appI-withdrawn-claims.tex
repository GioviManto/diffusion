\chapter{Withdrawn results}\label{app:withdrawn}

A thesis that reports only what survived is easier to read and harder to
trust. This appendix keeps the results that were claimed during the work and
then withdrawn, with the measurement that removed them, for two reasons. The
first is that a withdrawn result is evidence about the method that produced
it: each of these was withdrawn by a control that could have been run earlier,
and saying which control would have caught it is more useful than deleting the
episode. The second is that these numbers were circulated in earlier drafts,
and a reader who saw them is entitled to find out here what happened to them
rather than notice their silent absence.

The compendium's claim audit carries the full ledger, including claims that
were narrowed rather than dropped. What follows is the one that had been given
the most prominence.

\section{Pointwise accuracy and generative fidelity}
\label{app:withdrawn-generative}

\paragraph{What was claimed.} That pointwise denoising accuracy and generative
fidelity \emph{dissociate}: that an estimator can be an order of magnitude
better at predicting $\E[a \mid x]$ and simultaneously worse at generating
samples with the right innovation shape. Chapter~\ref{ch:em-results} called
this the central finding of the chapter.

\paragraph{Why it is withdrawn.} Every fit behind it used a fixed budget of
$40$ EM iterations. Section~\ref{sec:em-convergence} then measured that the
fitted innovation \emph{shape} can need on the order of $2000$ iterations to
settle, while the autoregressive coefficient is flat from about iteration
$25$. The generative metric is a shape statistic. So the comparison scored
converged estimators against unconverged ones on precisely the coordinate that
had not converged, and the ranking it produced is not attributable to the
estimators.

This is a confound, not a refutation --- the dissociation may well be real ---
but the experiment as run cannot distinguish it from the iteration budget, and
that is enough to withdraw the claim. It is recorded as withdrawn and
\emph{unreplaced}: no corrected version is reported here, because none has
been run at a budget that would settle the question.

\paragraph{The control that would have caught it.} Any convergence diagnostic
on the reported statistic itself. Monitoring $\rho$ --- the natural thing to
plot, and what was plotted --- certifies a run whose shape is still moving,
because the two coordinates converge at rates differing by a factor of eighty.
The lesson generalises past this claim: a convergence check must be run on the
quantity being reported, not on the parameter that is easiest to watch.

\paragraph{The numbers, for the record.} Generated-sample residual excess
kurtosis against a closed-form target of $1.9098$, mean over four seeds per
cell, under the corrected generation protocol (continuous log-uniform training
noise levels rather than five discrete ones; the two network-free arms are
bit-identical to the earlier protocol, and both trained networks improve under
it).

\noindent
The within-arm version was the sharpest form of the claim, and is worth
recording because it needs no comparison between estimators: at $N=2048$ the
global MLP's four per-seed generated-kurtosis values were $0.097$, $0.069$,
$0.019$ and $-0.002$ --- all indistinguishable from a Gaussian generated law
--- while its own pointwise error improved monotonically over the same range
of $N$. One estimator, disagreeing with itself across two notions of
correctness as its training data grows. The same $40$-iteration confound
applies to the arms it is compared against, and the proposed mechanism (a
better $L_2$ fit to the posterior mean is smoother, and a smoother denoiser
drives the reverse SDE toward a more Gaussian generated law) was never
measured directly.

\chapter{What Structure Buys, and Where It Stops Paying}
\label{ch:em-results}

Chapter~\ref{ch:em-method} built an estimator that is exact in its inference
step and approximate only in its numerical representation and its
optimisation budget. This chapter asks what that estimator is worth. Five
questions organise it, in the order the evidence answers them most cleanly.

\begin{quote}
How much does the structured estimator reduce denoising error at a given
amount of data; how much of that reduction survives against a baseline that is
given the locality but not the model; how much mixture capacity does the
estimator need; does the pointwise advantage survive when the estimated score
is used to \emph{generate}, by integrating the reverse process; and how far can
the Markov assumption itself be wrong before the structure stops helping?
\end{quote}

\noindent Four of the five are answered. The generative-fidelity question is
not, and the section that addresses it says why the experiment as run cannot
settle it. The capacity question is answered, but in the opposite direction to
the one an earlier and much smaller design suggested. The summary at the end of
the chapter states each of the five at the strength its evidence actually
supports.

Every comparison below fits the mixture kernel of \eqref{eq:em-mixture-kernel}
by the EM procedure of Chapter~\ref{ch:em-method} and compares it against
neural denoisers trained by ordinary denoising score matching on the same
noised data, with no access to the Markov factorisation, the linear kernel
form, or homogeneity across sites --- three structural facts the estimator is
given and the networks are not. Two network architectures recur throughout: a
fully connected MLP with no locality bias, and a weight-shared local network
with a fixed receptive field. The second is a \emph{locality-respecting}
baseline, not one matched to the estimator: EM--BP smooths over the whole chain
and has no radius of its own to match. The comparison measures the value of the
structure supplied, not a general claim about neural denoisers.

\section{Pointwise data efficiency}\label{sec:em-pointwise}

Under the structural assumptions of Section~\ref{sec:em-model}, at the data
budgets tested, the
structured estimator attains lower relative denoising error than the global
MLP by a factor of $\ratiolo$--$\ratiohi$ across the \nsizesused{} training-set
sizes (Table~\ref{tab:pointwise}, Figure~\ref{fig:em-sample-eff}). The factor
is \emph{not} monotone in the budget, and it is the largest of several
defensible aggregations of the same cells --- Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation}
gives the other four and the paired intervals. These figures are generated by
\codefile{tools/make\_tab\_efficiency.py} from the frozen outputs rather than
transcribed, so the table, the figure and this sentence cannot drift apart.

That comparison is against a network with no locality prior, and most of the
margin is attributable to that rather than to the estimator: against a
locality-respecting weight-shared baseline it narrows to
$\structratiolo$--$\structratiohi\times$, measured at the same headline
protocol and reported with its own scoping in
Section~\ref{sec:em-architecture}. The direction is consistent with the
locality results of Chapter~\ref{ch:ring} and the receptive-field argument of
Chapter~\ref{ch:gaussian}.

Both selections behind this comparison are made on a disjoint validation
split rather than on the test bundle: the network parameterisation
(predicting the noise or the clean signal) and, in a separate sweep, the
receptive-field radius. Validation selection agrees with the test-set choice
in 33 of 35 cells for the parameterisation and 54 of 60 for the two-axis
receptive-field sweep, and the quantities it moves are small --- the mean
advantage $11.65\times \to 11.66\times$, the receptive-field ratio
$3.846 \to 3.862$, both under $1.005\times$. The protocol is the defensible
one independently of the size of the correction it produces.

\section{How much of the margin is the architecture?}\label{sec:em-architecture}

A ratio against a fully connected network cannot separate knowing the Markov
structure from sharing weights across sites, and the second is the cheaper
explanation. This section reports the corrected, headline-protocol
measurement against a structure-aware baseline: the same twelve noise levels
and sixteen frozen seeds as Table~\ref{tab:pointwise}, every site scored (not
only the centre), and the network's architecture, radius and checkpoint all
selected on a validation bundle disjoint from test, deployed and provenance-
gated through \codefile{hpc/deploy\_clean.sh}.

\paragraph{Which architecture, and under what search.} Three candidates were
screened on $\screendevseeds$ development seeds, disjoint from the
$\structseeds$ used below: a weight-shared window head (which cannot propagate
past its radius however much data it sees), a dilated convolutional stack, and a
bidirectional message-passing network (both able, in principle, to propagate
across the whole chain). Each was run over its own hyperparameter grid --- a
shared learning-rate grid, both output parameterisations, and a common
checkpoint ladder from $\screenckptlo$ to $\screenckpthi$ steps ---
$\screenconfigs$ configurations in all, scored on the $\screenregion$ region.
\textbf{The window head won}, at radius $\screenwinnerradius$ and width
$\screenwinnerwidth$, with the $\screenrunnerup$ next
($\screenwinnerrisk$ against $\screenrunneruprisk$).

\input{../shared/sections/tab-screening}

What that does and does not license is worth stating, because the two are
easily confused. It is a \emph{model-selection} fact: under this search space,
this training protocol and these development seeds, the simplest candidate
scored best. It is not evidence that long-range propagation is intrinsically
useless here, that radius $\screenwinnerradius$ is the population-optimal
receptive field, or that a dilated or message-passing architecture could not
overtake it under different tuning. The one thing it does rule out is the
cheapest objection to the screen --- that the long-range arms lost because they
were starved. They were not: the dilated stack carries $\screenconvparams$
parameters against the window head's $\screenwindowparams$, an order of
magnitude more, and finished last of the three. \textbf{The confirmatory
comparison below is therefore EM--BP against a fixed-radius window head, the
strongest baseline \emph{this screen found}, not the strongest conceivable
amortised-inference baseline}, and that scoping is carried in every claim that
follows.

\input{../shared/sections/tab-structured}

A ratio against a fully connected network is not comparable to this one: a
large fraction of that headline margin disappears once locality and weight
sharing are supplied. What survives against a baseline that has them --- the
window head --- is $\structratiolo$--$\structratiohi\times$, all-site scored,
across the same four sizes as Table~\ref{tab:pointwise}. EM--BP is more
accurate in every one of the $\structcells$ scored cells; that count is
$\structseeds$ seeds $\times$ $\structsizes$ sizes $\times$ $\structregions$
scoring regions ($\structregionlist$), of which the table displays two, and it
is a descriptive diagnostic rather than a replication count --- the regions and
sizes share fitted models and training data, so the inferential unit behind each
displayed row is the $\structseeds$ paired seeds.

The ratio increases across the four sizes tested, from
$\structratiolo\times$ to $\structratiohi\times$. That is a description of four
measurements and nothing more is claimed from it. In particular it is not
evidence that the residual is non-architectural: an architecture with an
irreducible approximation floor $b^2$ would give
$R_{\text{window}}/R_{\text{EM}} = (b^2 + c_w/N)/(c_e/N)$, which grows linearly
in $N$ for an entirely architectural reason, and the raw risks in
Table~\ref{tab:structured} are consistent with exactly that --- EM--BP's risk
falls by a factor of about four and a half across the range while the window
head's falls by less than two. Different statistical rates or different
optimisation limits would produce the same signature. The direction is
measured; the mechanism is not.

Both arms choose their budget symmetrically, on a validation bundle disjoint
from test, \emph{within the budget grids on offer}: $\structcapem\%$ of EM--BP
cells and $\structcapwindow\%$ of window-head cells select the last rung of
their respective ladder. Selecting the last rung is not proof of convergence ---
it is a sign that validation error was still falling where the grid ran out, and
that a wider grid might have improved that arm further. So the reported values
compare the two procedures at stated, validation-selected computational budgets,
not at converged optima. Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation} reports the same ratio
under four aggregations of the same cells; every one of them, at every size,
exceeds $2\times$.

\section{How much mixture capacity is enough}\label{sec:em-capacity}

Enlarging $C$ in \eqref{eq:em-mixture-kernel} should help until the mixture can
represent the true innovation law and stop helping after. The design that tests
this pairs each capacity against $C=1$ --- a single Gaussian innovation, the
kernel the estimator would use if it did not model the innovation law at all ---
on identical training and evaluation data within each cell, at $\capseeds$
seeds and two training sizes, with three initialisations per cell and the
reported one selected on validation. Crucially the fits are run to their
plateau rather than to a fixed iteration count, because
Section~\ref{sec:em-convergence} measures the fitted shape as settling far later
than the correlation coefficient does, and shape is what capacity moves.

\input{../shared/sections/tab-capacity}

\noindent The result is that \textbf{no mixture capacity improves on a single
Gaussian innovation}, at either training size. The means are dominated by a few
large-magnitude seeds, so the sign count carries the claim: at $C=8$ the mixture
is ahead on only $\capcbestlo$ to $\capcbestwins$ of $\capseeds$ paired seeds,
with median paired differences of $\capmedhi$ and $\capmedlo$ per edge. The
same holds on schedule-level score risk.

The predeclared equivalence test sharpens this rather than softening it. The
region --- $|\Delta(\text{evidence/edge})| < 10^{-4}$ together with
$|\Delta R|/R < 1\%$ --- was fixed before the contrast was computed, precisely so
that ``$C=8$ and $C=16$ are equivalent'' could be asserted or refused rather
than inferred from a failure to reject. It is refused. At
$\nseq = \capheadsize$ the $C=8 \to C=16$ step is \capheadverdict{} against the
region and in the direction of $C=16$ being \emph{worse}: evidence
$\capheadlogev$ with interval $\capheadlogevci$, risk $\capheadrisk$ with
interval $\capheadriskci$, both lying wholly outside it. At
$\nseq = \capothersize$ it is \capotherverdict. So neither ``capacity saturates
by eight'' nor ``capacity keeps paying past eight'' is supported: past the
capacity the data supports, extra components cost.

\begin{remark}[Two things this does not isolate]\label{rmk:em-capacity-caveats}
Both point the same way, and both are reasons to read the table as a statement
about this estimator on this grid rather than about mixture capacity as such.
First, every cell at $C \geq \capcappedfrom$ stopped at the
$\capiterlimit$-iteration cap rather than at the convergence tolerance
($\capcappedcells$ of $\capcappedtotal$). The cap was placed at the measured
practical plateau --- a representative $C=16$ fit taken to $2000$ iterations has
per-edge gain $6.9\times10^{-8}$ at $400$, about $6\times10^{-9}$ by $800$, and
then does not move ($6.08\times10^{-9}$ at $1200$, $6.09\times10^{-9}$ at
$2000$), never reaching the strict tolerance at any practical count --- so the
residual asymmetry is small, but it is not zero, and higher capacities are the
ones it disfavours. Second, the share of fits whose narrowest component falls
below the grid's resolution floor of $\capsminfloor$ spacings climbs with
capacity, from $\capunresolvedone/\capunresolvedtotal$ at $C=1$ to
$\capunresolvedhi/\capunresolvedtotal$ at $C=16$. Part of what the larger
mixtures are fitting is therefore narrower than the grid can represent, which is
a property of the discretisation and not of the innovation law. Separating the
two would need a finer grid at fixed capacity, which is not run here.
\end{remark}

\noindent This supersedes a smaller earlier design --- six seeds, one training
size, one initialisation, a fixed forty-iteration budget --- which reported the
opposite sign and was read as capacity beginning to pay at $C=4$. That reading
does not reproduce at higher replication and longer budget, and the most likely
reason is the budget itself: forty iterations leaves the larger mixtures
under-fitted, which regularises them, and running them to their plateau exposes
what they do with the freedom. That explanation is consistent with the
convergence measurement of Section~\ref{sec:em-convergence} but is not
separately tested here.

\begin{remark}[Why the iteration budget, and not the capacity, has to be
controlled]\label{rmk:em-capacity-confound}
The reason the design above runs to a plateau is that a fixed budget cannot
distinguish the two. Section~\ref{sec:em-convergence} measures the innovation
shape as needing on the order of $2000$ iterations to settle while the
correlation coefficient settles by roughly $25$--$40$, a factor of about fifty;
applied to a fixed forty-iteration sweep, $93\%$ of $72$ seed-configurations had
not settled in the shape coordinate. Because a larger $C$ has more shape
parameters to move within the same budget, under-iteration alone produces a
curve that looks exactly like a capacity effect whether or not capacity is doing
anything, and a direct check attributes roughly $96\%$ of the apparent capacity
effect at that budget to convergence rate rather than to capacity. This is why
any quantity resting on fitted or generated \emph{shape} at a fixed budget --- in
particular the reverse-dynamics comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:em-generative}, which reuses forty-iteration fits --- is read
here as evidence about an under-converged operating point rather than about the
converged estimator.
\end{remark}

Runtime is close to flat across the capacity range, $13.5\to14.3$ seconds per
iteration from $C=2$ to $C=16$ while the number of free parameters grows
$6\to48$, confirming that the E-step's $O(nM^2)$ cost dominates and only the
M-step scales with $C$. Capacity is therefore nearly free to compute, which is
what makes the result above a statement about statistics rather than about
budget.

\section{Pointwise accuracy and generative fidelity}
\label{sec:em-generative}

Whether the pointwise advantage transfers to generation is not settled by
the experiments available here, and the reason is the budget rather than the
measurement. Every fit in this comparison used a fixed budget of $40$ EM
iterations, while Section~\ref{sec:em-convergence} measures the fitted
innovation shape --- which is precisely what the generative metric scores ---
as needing on the order of $2000$ iterations to settle. The comparison
therefore scores converged estimators against unconverged ones on the one
coordinate that had not converged, and a dissociation between pointwise and
generative quality cannot be separated from that asymmetry.

The dissociation may well be real, and the design cannot distinguish it from
its own iteration budget. No claim in either direction is made here. The
measured values, the within-arm form of the comparison and the convergence
diagnostic that settles it are recorded in the companion compendium's claim
audit. What would settle the question is the same correction that
Section~\ref{sec:em-capacity} applies: select both arms on a convergence
criterion rather than a fixed count, then compare.

\section{Where the Markov assumption stops paying}\label{sec:em-nonmarkov}

Every result so far assumes the clean chain is exactly first-order Markov.
Two controlled violations were measured against an exact reference (linear
Gaussian algebra for the contaminated model, so the estimator being wrong is
not confused with the reference being wrong): a rank-one global latent
shared across all sites at strength $\beta$, and a long-range coupling at
strength $\gamma$ added on top of the chain.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrrl@{}}
\toprule
violation & strength & CNN/EM-BP & Chow--Liu & reading \\
\midrule
global latent & $\beta=0$     & $11.4\to5.4$      & $-0.0009$ & Markov control \\
global latent & $\beta=0.5$   & $4.1\to3.2$       & $+0.0091$ & degraded, still winning \\
global latent & $\beta=1.0$   & $2.1\to3.1$       & $+0.0128$ & \textbf{never inverts} \\
long-range    & $\gamma=0$    & $25.4\to10.5$     & $+0.0014$ & Markov control \\
long-range    & $\gamma=0.10$ & $0.77\to1.32$     & $+0.0574$ & \textbf{inverts at low $t$} \\
long-range    & $\gamma=0.40$ & $0.47\to1.03$     & $+0.1010$ & \textbf{inverts across most of it} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\noindent The two violations behave qualitatively differently, and the
difference is principled rather than incidental. A global latent shared
across every site makes the score residual \emph{exactly} rank one, by a
Woodbury identity, and over the range tested a chain-structured estimator
accommodates that correction within its own fitted coefficients: the fitted
$\alpha$ rises and the fitted innovation kurtosis flattens as $\beta$ grows,
tracking the contamination it is misattributing to the chain. This is
partial accommodation over a measured range, not an exact representation ---
a first-order Markov family does not contain the contaminated law, and what
the numbers show is that the residual it cannot represent stays small enough
not to invert the advantage. Long-range
coupling is only \emph{approximately} low rank, and a chain family cannot
represent it at all: the advantage is gone by $\gamma\approx0.05$--$0.10$ and
the structured estimator's own relative error reaches $57\%$ at high noise
and the strongest tested coupling. The minimum CNN/EM-BP ratio over the full
noise schedule never falls below $2.08$ for the rank-one violation at any
strength up to $\beta=1.0$ --- where half the marginal variance is a shared
global constant --- and falls to $0.47$ for the long-range one. The Chow--Liu
deviation column corroborates the mechanism independently: the fitted chain
stays close to the best possible Markov approximation of the truth under
rank-one contamination and departs from it under long-range coupling,
exactly where a chain can no longer represent what generated the data. The
same qualitative split holds when the innovation law is Laplace rather than
Gaussian: the rank-one violation still never inverts the advantage, with the
minimum ratio at $\beta=1.0$ equal to $1.45$ against an exact non-Markov
reference.

\begin{remark}
This extends the lesson of Chapter~\ref{ch:laplace}: there, what broke
under a harder innovation law was the \emph{representability of messages} in
a finite-dimensional family, while the tree, the two-sweep schedule, and the
score--posterior identity survived unconditionally. Here, the further
question is what breaks when the graph is not a tree to begin with. The
answer is not "misspecification degrades the advantage" uniformly --- it is
that a chain-structured estimator tolerates the one violation it can
absorb into its own parameters and fails on the one it structurally cannot
represent, and the two are not interchangeable amounts of "how wrong the
model is": they are different in kind.
\end{remark}

\section{Shape is intrinsically harder to identify than correlation}
\label{sec:em-information}

Section~\ref{sec:em-convergence} found that the innovation shape converges
far slower than the autoregressive coefficient under EM. A direct
information-theoretic measurement gives a structural reason.

\paragraph{What is computed.} The innovation law is a generalised Gaussian
$p(\varepsilon) \propto \exp(-|\varepsilon/s|^{\beta})$, with $s$ fixed by
$\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = q = 1-\alpha^2$ at every $\beta$, so
$\beta$ moves the fourth moment at \emph{exactly fixed} variance and is a
genuine shape coordinate rather than a reparameterised scale. For a
three-parameter vector $\theta = (\alpha, q, \beta)$, Fisher's identity
\eqref{eq:em-fisher} supplies the exact per-chain score of the marginal
log-likelihood from one forward--backward pass, and the information matrix
is estimated in \emph{outer-product} form, $J = D^{-1}\sum_d g_d g_d^\T$
over $D=512$ chains of $n=32$ sites, at $M=401$, $A=8$. This is the
outer-product estimator, not the observed negative Hessian, and it is an
estimate at the true parameter rather than at a fitted one. The reported
quantity is the \emph{efficient} information
$I_{\beta\cdot(\alpha,q)} = 1/(J^{-1})_{\beta\beta}$, which is what remains
about $\beta$ once the nuisance coordinates must be estimated too --- equal
to the diagonal entry only when the coordinates are orthogonal and strictly
smaller otherwise. The gap between $J_{\beta\beta}$ and this quantity is
recorded per cell as the nuisance cost, and the condition number of $J$ is
recorded alongside it, since the inversion is where a near-singular $J$
would show up.

\paragraph{What it shows.} The efficient information for $\beta$ decays two
to three orders of magnitude faster with diffusion noise than the
information for $\alpha$ itself. Reading the loss between the smallest and
largest diffusion time tested ($t = 0.05$ against $t = 1.6$):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrr@{}}
\toprule
true $\beta$ & info loss, $\alpha$ & info loss, $q$ & info loss, $\beta$ (shape) \\
\midrule
1.0 & $78\times$ & $235\times$ & $\mathbf{8{,}738\times}$ \\
1.5 & $83\times$ & $459\times$ & $\mathbf{64{,}227\times}$ \\
3.0 & $56\times$ & $522\times$ & $\mathbf{68{,}460\times}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\noindent This is not merely a restatement of the convergence finding: it is
a property of the noised likelihood itself, independent of any optimiser,
and it explains \emph{why} the shape coordinate is slow --- there is
structurally less information about it in noised data at any fixed sample
size, at every diffusion time past the smallest ones. It is also the reason
the capacity sweep of Section~\ref{sec:em-capacity} is exactly the kind of
measurement where an insufficient iteration budget is dangerous: the
coordinate that is hardest to move is also the one with the least signal per
observation.

Two limits on how far these figures should be pushed. They are ratios of
outer-product estimates at $D=512$ chains, so the leading digits carry
Monte Carlo error and the right reading of $64{,}227$ is ``four to five
orders of magnitude'', not a five-significant-figure measurement. And the
comparison is across the tested noise grid rather than a limit: the decay
is measured between $t=0.05$ and $t=1.6$, and how it continues past the
schedule's end is not established here. What the table supports is the
direction and the order --- shape information collapses far faster than
correlation information --- which is the claim the rest of the chapter
rests on.

\section{Scope not covered here}

The estimator of Chapter~\ref{ch:em-method} was also extended, within the
same research package, to video sequences and to natural-image patches, and
to a wavelet-domain hidden-Markov-tree generalisation of the chain structure
used throughout this thesis. Those extensions are reported in the package's
own notebooks rather than reproduced here. The image comparison in
particular fits the two kernel families with different and not directly
comparable factories, so it does not support a ratio between them, and none
is quoted in this thesis.

\section{Summary}

Four of the five questions this chapter opened with are answered, and one is
left open.

\paragraph{Pointwise, against an unstructured network.} At equal training-set
size the structured estimator attains relative denoising error lower than the
MLP by a factor of $\ratiolo$--$\ratiohi$, across \nsizesused{} sizes and
\nseedsused{} replicates. This is a ratio of errors at fixed data, not a ratio
of the data required to reach a fixed error: the two curves do not cross in the
range measured, so no data-equivalence factor is quoted.

\paragraph{Pointwise, against a structure-aware network.} A large fraction of
that margin is attributable to the MLP's lack of any locality prior rather than
to the estimator. Against a weight-shared window head, at the same headline
protocol and every site scored, the advantage is
$\structratiolo$--$\structratiohi\times$, and it increases across the four sizes
tested rather than falling. The increase is a description of those four
measurements; an architecture with an approximation floor would produce the same
signature, and this design does not separate the two
(Section~\ref{sec:em-architecture}). The window head was itself the winner of a
screen against two architectures built to propagate further across the chain,
which bounds what the baseline is --- the strongest this screen found, not the
strongest conceivable.

\paragraph{Capacity.} No mixture capacity improves on a single Gaussian
innovation, at either training size, once fits are run to their plateau rather
than to a fixed iteration count. The predeclared equivalence test refuses
``saturates by eight'' as well: at $\nseq = \capheadsize$ the $C=8 \to C=16$
step is resolved against the region in the direction of $C=16$ being worse.
Two things are not isolated --- the iteration cap and the grid's resolution
floor, both of which disfavour the larger mixtures
(Remark~\ref{rmk:em-capacity-caveats}) --- so the claim is about this estimator
on this grid, not about mixture capacity in general.

\paragraph{Misspecification.} The Markov structure the estimator relies on is
not a single quantity of ``how much structure''. It survives a violation it can
absorb into its own parameters --- a rank-one global latent, where the advantage
never inverts even when half the marginal variance is a shared constant --- and
fails, well before generic intuition about misspecification would suggest, on a
long-range coupling it structurally cannot represent.

\paragraph{Generation: open.} Whether the pointwise advantage transfers to
generation is not resolved here. The comparison available scores converged
estimators against unconverged ones on the single coordinate that had not
converged, so Section~\ref{sec:em-generative} withdraws the reading rather than
reversing it. The correction is the one applied to capacity above: select on a
convergence criterion, then compare.

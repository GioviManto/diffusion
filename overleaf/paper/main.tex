\begin{abstract}
Over an unrestricted function class, the global minimiser of the denoising objective that trains a
diffusion model is not the score of the data distribution: it is the score of the \emph{empirical}
distribution, a sum of point masses at the training samples smoothed by the forward noise. A model
attaining that target exactly, integrated to zero noise, would reproduce its training set and
nothing else, yet diffusion models generate new data. Deciding what a trained network has actually
learned requires comparing it against the score it should have learned --- and for essentially
every distribution of interest that target is unavailable, so a network's error cannot be
separated from the difficulty of its target. Tractable analytical models exist, but the most
common are Gaussian, whose linear scores cannot probe how an approximation handles genuinely
nonlinear posterior structure.

We study a family where the target is computable and non-trivial: a first-order Markov chain on
$\Reals^{\nsites}$ with continuous, non-Gaussian transitions, variance-matched so that innovation
families differ only beyond second moments. Coordinatewise Ornstein--Uhlenbeck noising multiplies
the prior by \emph{unary} factors, adding no edges among the latent variables, so the posterior
factor graph is still the prior's chain --- a tree, on which sum-product is exact. The score is
therefore available as an exact functional recursion for a correlated, non-Gaussian model, and
numerically to a validated tolerance once the continuous messages are discretised.

We then drop the assumption that the chain's parameters are known and learn them from noised
sequences alone. Fisher's identity supplies the exact gradient of the marginal log-likelihood from
one complete forward--backward pass, with no differentiation through the recursion, so both gradient ascent
and expectation--maximisation are available; for Gaussian innovations the maximisation step is
closed form and the two routes reach the same optimum. We prove that single-Gaussian message
closure computes \emph{exactly} the LMMSE estimator of the covariance-matched Gaussian model, so
the gap between it and full sum-product is precisely the price of discarding everything beyond
second moments. Estimating a transition \emph{density} rather than its parameters exposes a
convergence-rate asymmetry between the correlation and shape coordinates, to which two mechanisms
contribute: the innovation mixture's own latent label, which makes the shape slow even on clean
transitions, and the observation channel, which compounds it and removes far more Fisher
information about shape than about correlation.

Against an \emph{unstructured} network trained by denoising score matching on identical data, with
the optimisation budget of both arms selected on a held-out bundle, the structured estimator
attains $\ratiolo$--$\ratiohi\times$ lower pointwise denoising error, at equal
training-set size, across \nsizesused{} sizes --- a ratio of errors at fixed data, not a reduction
in the data required to reach a fixed error. A large fraction of that margin disappears once the
baseline is given locality and weight sharing: against a window head, selected by screening over
three candidate architectures and measured at the same headline protocol, all-site scored, the
advantage falls to $\structratiolo$--$\structratiohi\times$, increasing across the sizes tested
rather than falling. The comparison is
deliberately asymmetric in prior information --- the estimator is handed the Markov factorisation,
the autoregressive form and a low-dimensional innovation family, the networks none of them --- so
it measures the worth of that structure on this family, not a general ranking of the two
approaches.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Generative diffusion produces data $\va\in\Reals^{\nsites}$ from a distribution $\Pz$ known only
through samples, by noising and then reversing \citep{sohl2015,ho2020,song2021}. Reversal is
driven by the score $\score(\vx,t)=\nabla_{\vx}\log\Pt(\vx)$, which is learned by regression
against the posterior mean of the clean signal.

\paragraph{The puzzle that motivates this.} A network never sees $\Pz$. It sees $\nseq$ samples,
the empirical measure $\hat P_0 = \nseq^{-1}\sum_\mu \delta(\cdot - \va^\mu)$, and over an
unrestricted function class the global minimiser of the denoising objective is the score of
\emph{that}. A model attaining it exactly, integrated to zero noise, would have a reverse process
whose fixed points are the training samples and would generate nothing else. Diffusion models
nonetheless generate new data, and when they memorise rather than generalise is an active question
\citep{biroli2023,biroli2024dynamical}.

Measuring it is the difficulty: deciding what a network has learned means comparing it against the
score it \emph{should} have learned, and for realistic data that object is unavailable, so a large
error may indicate a poor model or a hard target with nothing to distinguish them. The tractable
alternatives are poor probes of \emph{structure}. A Gaussian score is linear, so any question about
exploiting correlation has a degenerate answer; a mixture score is nonlinear through its
responsibilities --- in the mixture index, not in any dependence between coordinates.

\paragraph{Why a Markov chain.} Two reasons. Diffusion is applied to sequential data --- video,
audio, time series --- so a model of correlation along a sequence is the relevant caricature. And
studying how structure is exploited requires data that has structure, of a kind one can dial. A
first-order Markov chain with continuous non-Gaussian transitions supplies both, and the
variance-matching of \S\ref{sec:setting} makes the innovation family a controlled knob: Gaussian,
Laplace, Student, uniform and bimodal chains share a covariance exactly and differ only beyond it.

\paragraph{Why it is tractable.} Coordinatewise noising contributes one \emph{unary} factor per
site. Unary factors reweight node potentials without creating edges among the latent variables, so
conditioning leaves the factor graph a chain --- a tree, on which sum-product returns exact
marginals. The exact score is therefore computable for a correlated, non-Gaussian model, and the
only obstruction is that the messages are functions on $\Reals$ and must be represented. The
Gaussian case, where the answer is known in closed form, is what validates that representation
before it is used where the answer is not known.

\paragraph{The question this makes askable.} If the data is Markov, is it \emph{easier to learn}?
Sum-product reaches the exact score through very simple operations, and it is Markovianity that
permits this. So we drop the assumption that the chain's parameters are known: keep the recursion,
treat the kernel as unknown, start from a random initialisation, and ascend the likelihood on a
finite sample, with Fisher's identity supplying the exact gradient. The resulting estimator is
compared against networks trained by denoising score matching on identical data.

Rather than survey what the model can do, we ask one question --- \emph{given only sequences seen
through the noising channel, what can be recovered?} --- at three levels of difficulty, deriving
the answer at each before measuring it:

The three share one model, one channel and one estimator, differing only in how much of the
transition is withheld; each adds exactly one difficulty to the one below it.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item A denoising reference for a continuous, non-Gaussian, correlated model, with truncation and
      quadrature error separated and measured, validated against a closed form and against
      brute-force enumeration (\S\ref{sec:rung0}).
\item An identification of what single-Gaussian message closure computes here: exactly the LMMSE
      estimator of the covariance-matched Gaussian model (Proposition~\ref{prop:lmmse}), proved
      directly rather than argued asymptotically.
\item Estimation of the kernel from noised sequences alone, with the exact gradient from one
      complete forward--backward pass and no differentiation through the recursion
      (\S\ref{sec:rung1}--\S\ref{sec:rung2}).
\item A measurement of what supplying that structure is worth against trained denoisers given none
      of it, both arms' budget selected on held-out data (\S\ref{sec:networks}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Diffusion and score matching.} The construction is due to \citet{sohl2015}, developed
by \citet{ho2020} and given its continuous-time form by \citet{song2021}; training targets the
score by denoising score matching \citep{hyvarinen2005,vincent2011}, and that the regression
target is a posterior mean is Tweedie's formula \citep{robbins1956,miyasawa1961,efron2011}.

\paragraph{Statistical mechanics, and the memorisation question.} The high-dimensional behaviour of
these models is analysed as a phase-transition problem \citep{biroli2023,biroli2024dynamical,
achilli2026speciation}. The puzzle above is attacked within it through training dynamics, where the
time to memorise grows with sample size while the time to generalise does not
\citep{bonnaire2025memorize,favero2025bigger}; through the learned representation, geometry-adaptive
rather than sample-specific \citep{kadkhodaie2024generalization}; through the spectrum of the
empirical score \citep{achilli2024losing}; and in solvable settings
\citep{cui2025solvable,raya2023spontaneous,ghio2024sampling}, with
\citet{garnierbrun2026biased} exhibiting a phase where test loss still falls while samples drift
anomalously close to the training set. Every one measures a network against a target unavailable in
closed form --- which is what the model below supplies.

\paragraph{Tractable structured data, and architectures that implement inference.} Hierarchical
generative models supply tractable non-Gaussian families with a phase structure
\citep{sclocchi2025phase}, and the same construction can be run backwards to read latent structure
off real data \citep{sclocchi2025probing} or to date the emergence of compositional generalisation
during training \citep{favero2025compositional}. Two results bear directly on what we leave open:
\citet{mei2024unet}
reads U-Nets on such models as implementing belief propagation, and
\citet{garnierbrun2024transformers} shows transformers learning the corresponding inference on
hierarchically filtered data. Both concern \emph{tree}-structured data, where the natural depth is
the tree's. A chain is a degenerate tree --- depth equal to its length, width two --- so the
architectural implication differs, which is what \S\ref{sec:networks} sets up rather than settles.

\paragraph{Graphical models and inference.} Sum-product and its exactness on trees are standard
\citep{pearl1988,kschischang2001,mezard2009,wainwright2008}; on a chain the recursions are
forward--backward \citep{baum1970,rabiner1989}, reducing to Kalman and RTS in the Gaussian case
\citep{kalman1960,rauch1965}. The estimation machinery is EM \citep{dempster1977}, its
conditional-maximisation variants \citep{wu1983,meng1993}, and Fisher's identity
\citep{fisher1925,louis1982,cappe2005}.

\paragraph{What is not new here.} The numerics are classical too: messages on a grid, integrated by
quadrature, is Kitagawa's non-Gaussian state-space smoother \citep{kitagawa1987}, alongside
Gaussian-sum \citep{alspach1972}, expectation-propagation \citep{minka2001} and Monte Carlo
\citep{gordon1993} alternatives \citep[see][]{sarkka2013}. Grid BP is Kitagawa's method at each
diffusion time; Rung~1 is maximum likelihood for a discretised continuous-state HMM, whose
identifiability under noisy observation is itself studied \citep{allman2009}. The contribution is
not the estimator but what this model permits: a score known across the whole schedule at a
controlled tolerance, turning ``what does an approximation cost'' into a measurement.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Notation.} $\nsites$ is the chain length, which is also the ambient dimension, and
$\nseq$ is the number of observed sequences; the diffusion literature's $n$ and $d$ translate as
$n\to\nseq$, $d\to\nsites$. Vectors are bold lowercase and matrices bold uppercase, so
$\va\in\Reals^{\nsites}$ is a whole clean chain and $a_i$ its $i$-th site. $\kernel$ is always the
transition kernel and $\ngrid$ always the number of quadrature points
(Appendix~\ref{app:notation}).

\input{../shared/sections/model}

\paragraph{Conditioning preserves the chain.} Conditioning \eqref{eq:prior} on $\vx$ gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:posterior}
P(\va \mid \vx,t) \;\propto\;
\mu(a_1)\prod_{i=2}^{\nsites} \kernel(a_i\mid a_{i-1})
\prod_{i=1}^{\nsites} \like(x_i \mid a_i),
\qquad
\like(x_i\mid a_i) \propto \exp\!\left[-\frac{(x_i - e^{-t}a_i)^2}{2\Dt}\right].
\end{equation}
The likelihood factors are \emph{unary}: the forward noise is independent across coordinates, so
each observation touches one latent variable. They reweight node potentials and add no edges, so
the posterior factor graph is the prior's chain (drawn in Appendix~\ref{app:bp}).

A \emph{tree} is a connected acyclic graph --- there is exactly one path between any two nodes. A
chain is the simplest tree, the one whose nodes have degree at most two. On a tree, sum-product
returns the exact marginals \citep{pearl1988,kschischang2001,mezard2009}: with
\begin{align}
\label{eq:messages}
\lmsg_{i}(a_i) &\propto \int \mathrm{d}a_{i-1}\;
\kernel(a_i\mid a_{i-1})\,\like(x_{i-1}\mid a_{i-1})\,\lmsg_{i-1}(a_{i-1}), \nonumber\\[-2pt]
\rmsg_{i}(a_i) &\propto \int \mathrm{d}a_{i+1}\;
\kernel(a_{i+1}\mid a_i)\,\like(x_{i+1}\mid a_{i+1})\,\rmsg_{i+1}(a_{i+1}),
\end{align}
initialised by $\lmsg_1 = \mu$ and $\rmsg_{\nsites}\equiv 1$, the single-site marginal is
$P(a_i\mid \vx,t) \propto \lmsg_i(a_i)\like(x_i\mid a_i)\rmsg_i(a_i)$ and the pairwise marginal on
edge $(i-1,i)$ is $\propto \lmsg_{i-1}\like(x_{i-1}\mid a_{i-1})\kernel(a_i\mid
a_{i-1})\like(x_i\mid a_i)\rmsg_i$. The local likelihood is absorbed \emph{before} propagation, so
$\like(x_i\mid a_i)$ appears exactly once in each marginal. These are the forward--backward
recursions of hidden Markov models \citep{baum1970,rabiner1989} written for a continuous state;
when everything is Gaussian they reduce to the Kalman filter and RTS smoother
\citep{kalman1960,rauch1965}.


Two cautions, both expanded in Appendix~\ref{app:exactness}. Sum-product is exact on the tree,
but a message is a \emph{function} on $\Reals$ --- forced by the state space, not chosen --- so an
implementation must represent one. The truncated grid we use makes the recursion exact only
for the chain-shaped factor model that grid defines, not for a normalised finite-state kernel:
the sampled columns are not renormalised (Appendix~\ref{app:exactness}). The distance to the
continuum recursion is what \S\ref{sec:rung0} measures. And determinism here is conditional --- on
a dataset and a grid the inference is deterministic, but every reported risk, ratio and
information estimate is an average over sampled sequences at finitely many seeds.

\paragraph{Cost.} The discretised posterior has $\ngrid^{\nsites}$ joint configurations, so one
site marginal by direct summation fixes that site and sums over the other $\nsites-1$:
$O(\ngrid^{\nsites-1})$ per marginal, about $10^{80}$ terms at $\ngrid=401$, $\nsites=32$. The recursion
replaces it with $2(\nsites-1)$ matrix--vector products: $O(\nsites\ngrid^{2})$ time per sequence
and $O(\nsites\ngrid)$ storage for the messages, plus one shared $O(\ngrid^{2})$ transition
matrix. Exponential in the chain length becomes linear in it and quadratic in the representation
size (Appendix~\ref{app:bp}).


\section{Rung 0 --- the kernel is known}
\label{sec:rung0}

With $\kernel$ given, \eqref{eq:messages} returns the posterior mean and hence, through
\eqref{eq:score}, the score. Two things must be established before anything is built on it: that
the discretisation is controlled, and what the standard Gaussian approximation actually computes.

\paragraph{The discretisation, controlled on two axes.} Two error sources are separated by
construction: \emph{truncation}, mass outside $[-\halfwidth,\halfwidth]$ discarded rather than
transported, controlled by $\halfwidth$ and essentially independent of $\ngrid$; and
\emph{quadrature}, second order in $\gridh$, and no better than second order for the Laplace
family, whose innovation has a discontinuous derivative at the origin. Each is measured with a
diagnostic that isolates it, since a resolution sweep at fixed $\halfwidth$ cannot rule out
truncation and a domain sweep at fixed $\ngrid$ cannot rule out quadrature
(Figure~\ref{fig:grid}, Appendix~\ref{app:numerics}). The working configuration $\ngrid=401$,
$\halfwidth=8$ is read off those curves, and puts both diagnostics far below every effect
reported here.

\paragraph{Validation where the answer is known.} Take $\innov=\normal{0}{\ivar}$. Then $\va$ is
jointly Gaussian with $(\Sig_0)_{ij}=\corr^{|i-j|}$, and so is $\vx$, with
$\Sig_t = e^{-2t}\Sig_0 + \Dt \Id$, giving
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gaussian-closed}
\pmean{\va} = e^{-t}\Sig_0\Sig_t^{-1}\vx,
\qquad
\score(\vx,t) = -\Sig_t^{-1}\vx .
\end{equation}
The clean precision $\Sig_0^{-1}$ is tridiagonal, and the posterior precision
$(e^{-2t}/\Dt)\Id + \Sig_0^{-1}$ stays tridiagonal at every $t$: being Markov is a statement about
the precision, not the covariance, which is dense. At $\ngrid=401$, $\halfwidth=8$ the discretised
recursion reproduces \eqref{eq:gaussian-closed} to $9.2\times10^{-15}$ relative, and agrees with
brute-force enumeration of the discretised posterior --- summing over all $\ngrid^{\nsites}$
configurations on a short chain, sharing no code with the message passing --- to
$1.6\times10^{-14}$ on posterior means and $1.8\times10^{-15}$ on the log-evidence. Neither check
involves non-Gaussian machinery: the pipeline is verified where the answer is known before being
used where it is not.

\paragraph{What the standard Gaussian approximation computes.} A common approximation propagates
only the first two moments of each message. Under $a_i = \corr a_{i-1} + \varepsilon_i$, a message
with mean $m$ and variance $v$ transforms as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:closure}
\text{forward:}\quad m \mapsto \corr m,\;\; v \mapsto \corr^2 v + \ivar ;
\qquad
\text{backward:}\quad m \mapsto m/\corr,\;\; v \mapsto (v+\ivar)/\corr^2 ,
\end{equation}
the backward map being the forward one inverted because that recursion integrates over the output
variable (it requires $\corr\neq0$). The propagation uses only the mean and variance of $\innov$.

\input{../shared/sections/prop-lmmse}

We call \eqref{eq:lmmse} the \emph{Gaussian second-order baseline}. On this model, message
closure, moment projection and outright replacement of the prior by its covariance-matched
Gaussian all coincide; they separate for richer message families. We do not call the method AMP: a
degree-two chain supplies no large-degree central-limit justification for that terminology, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:lmmse} is proved directly rather than argued asymptotically, by induction
over the two recursions (Appendix~\ref{app:lmmse}, with Remark~\ref{rem:lmmse-hyp} on why each
hypothesis is needed and on the fact that the grid plays no part in the statement). The zero-mean
hypothesis is load-bearing in practice as well as in the proof: at $\E[a]=1.3$ the estimator
\eqref{eq:lmmse} departs from the true LMMSE by $0.65$ in maximum absolute value.

Proposition~\ref{prop:lmmse} is what makes the gap between \eqref{eq:lmmse} and grid BP
interpretable: it is not an artefact of a message representation, it is exactly the price of
replacing the prior by its covariance twin. That gap locates where non-Gaussianity matters
(Figure~\ref{fig:closure}), and it shrinks as $t$ grows --- because the noised marginal $\Pt$
Gaussianises and Tweedie's prefactor $\alpha_t/\Delta_t$ attenuates the score's sensitivity to
structure beyond second moments, \emph{not} because the latent posterior becomes Gaussian. It does
not: as $t\to\infty$ the observation becomes uninformative and $P(\va\mid\vx,t)$ approaches the
prior, the non-Gaussian object at issue.

\section{Rung 1 --- recovering two numbers}
\label{sec:rung1}

Now $\kernel$ is unknown. Take the simplest case first: Gaussian innovations, so the kernel is
$\kernelth(a'\mid a)=\normal{a'-\corr a}{\ivar}$ and $\params=(\corr,\ivar)$ is two numbers. We
observe only noised sequences $\vx^\mu$, $\mu=1,\dots,\nseq$, and write
$\Lik(\params)=\sum_\mu \log P_{t,\params}(\vx^\mu)$ for the marginal log-likelihood.

\paragraph{The gradient needs no backpropagation.} The obvious route is gradient ascent on $\Lik$. That looks
expensive --- $\Lik$ is defined through the recursion, so differentiating it appears to require
backpropagating through $2(\nsites-1)$ message updates. It does not. Fisher's identity
\citep{fisher1925,louis1982,cappe2005} gives

\input{../shared/sections/prop-fisher}

\noindent
Sum-product is not a computation graph to backpropagate through; it supplies the conditional
expectation, and one forward--backward pass yields the exact gradient of the discretised marginal
likelihood (Remark~\ref{rem:fisher-scope}). We verify \eqref{eq:fisher} against finite differences
to $\sim10^{-9}$; that check shares the forward recursion with what it validates, so the
independent controls are brute-force posterior enumeration on a tiny grid and the closed-form
Gaussian gradient (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

$\Xis$ is the continuum analogue of Baum--Welch's expected transition counts \citep{baum1970}, and
it is sufficient \emph{for the current $\Qfun$} only --- not a sufficient statistic of the data.
Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} states the conditions and the cost.

\paragraph{Two routes to the same place.} Equation~\eqref{eq:fisher} licenses gradient ascent;
EM instead increases $\Qfun$. For the Gaussian kernel the maximisation step is closed form ---
profiling $\Qfun$ gives the belief-weighted Yule--Walker equations
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:yw}
\corr \;=\; S_{01}/S_{00},
\qquad
\ivar \;=\; (S_{11} - \corr\,S_{01})/W ,
\end{equation}
with $S_{ab}=\sum_{kl}\Xis_{kl}\,\gridpt{k}^{a}\gridpt{l}^{b}$ and $W=\sum_{kl}\Xis_{kl}$ --- so
this rung has \emph{exact} EM, not a generalised variant. The derivation is
Appendix~\ref{app:learning}.

Both routes are implemented and neither dominates. On this smooth kernel gradient ascent reaches
EM's optimum to $1.6\times10^{-9}$ nats, but it needs a tuned step size and larger steps diverge;
EM needs no step size and its ascent is monotone by construction \citep{dempster1977,wu1983}.
That is the whole content of the comparison, and it is why every rung above uses EM. The one place
gradient ascent wins is a kernel whose $\Qfun$-maximiser is a lattice artefact of the
discretisation, which is a statement about the grid rather than about the algorithms
(Appendix~\ref{app:learning}).

\paragraph{Identifiability, at this rung, is a theorem.} \eqref{eq:yw} inverts the map
$(\corr,\ivar)\mapsto(S_{01}/S_{00},\dots)$ explicitly, and Gaussian convolution at finite $t$ is
injective on distributions --- the characteristic function acquires a factor
$e^{-\Dt\|\xi\|^2/2}$, which has no zeros, and $e^{-t}>0$ --- so $\Pt$ determines $\Pz$ and
$\Pz$ determines $(\corr,\ivar)$. Nothing is assumed here. That changes at Rung 2, and the change
is worth marking rather than burying: see \S\ref{sec:rung2}.

\paragraph{What is measured.} Recovery of $(\corr,\ivar)$ from initialisations away from the
truth, and the rate at which the error falls with $\nseq$. Theory predicts $\nseq^{-1/2}$, the
ordinary parametric rate, since this is maximum likelihood in a correctly specified
two-parameter model. Over $\nseq=64$ to $1024$ at sixteen replicates, the innovation-variance
error falls from $2.7\times10^{-2}$ to $6.4\times10^{-3}$, a fitted $\log$--$\log$ slope of
$-0.52$ against the predicted $-0.50$ ($r=-0.99$, monotone). The $\corr$ error falls monotonically
over the same range but more slowly, at $-0.27$; we report the discrepancy rather than explain it,
since at these budgets $\corr$ is already accurate to $5\times10^{-3}$ and we have not established
that the residual is sampling error rather than a floor.

\section{Rung 2 --- recovering a density}
\label{sec:rung2}

Free the innovation shape. Let
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kernel}
\kernelth(a'\mid a) \;=\; \sum_{c=1}^{\ncomp} \pi_c \,
\normal{a' - \corr a - \nu_c}{s_c^2},
\end{equation}
in which \textbf{both} the autoregressive coefficient and the innovation density are free. At
$\ncomp=8$ this is $\corr$, seven weights after the simplex constraint, eight locations and eight
scales. Two things change at once, and they are worth separating.

\paragraph{A second latent variable.} At Rung 1 the complete-data problem --- knowing $\va$, fit
$\kernel$ --- was a two-parameter regression with a closed-form solution. Here, knowing $\va$ is
not enough: the complete-data problem is itself a mixture fit, carrying the component label as a
second latent variable, so the maximisation step has no closed form. We alternate the
$(\pi,\nu,s^2)$ and $\corr$ blocks, each closed form given the other, for up to sixteen inner
sweeps or until the per-edge gain in $\Qfun$ falls below $10^{-8}$. The algorithm is therefore \textbf{generalised EM} of ECM type
\citep{dempster1977,meng1993}: every step increases $\Qfun$ without maximising it, which preserves
monotone ascent \citep{wu1983} but not the exact-maximiser property. We do not call it exact EM.

\paragraph{Misspecification.} When the truth is Laplace and the family is the mixture
\eqref{eq:kernel}, what is recovered is a \emph{density}, not a parameter vector, and the mixture
is a flexible representation of it rather than the truth in disguise. Identifiability weakens
correspondingly: finite mixtures are identifiable up to label permutation on the interior of the
parameter set --- all $\pi_c>0$, the pairs $(\nu_c,s_c)$ distinct \citep{teicher1963,yakowitz1968}
--- which is exactly where an over-specified $\ncomp$ need not stay. The condition is monitored
(minimum component mass, minimum width in grid spacings) rather than assumed. This sits between
Rung 1's theorem and what could be said for a kernel left unconstrained in shape, where even
conditional identifiability would not be available.

\paragraph{Why the fourth cumulant, and not some other summary.} A density needs a scalar summary
to be tracked, and the choice is forced rather than free. Every innovation family used here is
symmetric, so all odd cumulants vanish: $\kappa_3=0$ identically. The variance is matched by
construction, $\ivar=1-\corr^2$, so $\kappa_2$ is fixed across families and carries no
discriminating information at all. The first cumulant that \emph{can} differ between a Laplace
innovation and its covariance twin is therefore the fourth. Excess kurtosis is not a preference;
at matched variance and under symmetry it is the leading coordinate in which non-Gaussianity can
appear.

\paragraph{The coordinates do not converge at the same rate.} This is the substantive finding of
the rung, and it is a statement about the optimiser rather than about information.

\begin{remark}[Two different slownesses, kept apart]
\label{rem:rates}
On \emph{clean} chains, with no missing information whatsoever, $\corr$ and $\ivar$ are converged
after a single maximisation step --- with clean data $\Xis$ is the empirical pair distribution and
does not depend on $\params$ at all --- while the innovation shape still takes tens of iterations.
That slowness belongs to the mixture's inner latent label, the extra difficulty introduced above. It is not caused by the channel.

Through the channel both coordinates slow down further, and \emph{that} is the channel's doing:
it is the missing-information mechanism of \citet{dempster1977} appearing exactly where the theory
puts it, in the convergence rate rather than in the attainable accuracy.

The two effects are separable and we separate them, because conflating them produces a false
conclusion --- an apparent \emph{information} penalty that is really a rate at a fixed iteration
budget. Run long enough, both arms reach the same fit within the resolution of the comparison.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{The score is recovered faster than the density that induces it.} Reporting recovery
through the induced score alone is generous: the channel has already blurred the innovation law by
the time the score is evaluated. Measured directly, by Hellinger distance between kernel columns,
the density lags. Across $\nseq=32$ to $2048$ the relative score error falls by a factor of $4.5$
while the transition distance falls by $2$ --- a sixty-fourfold increase in data buys much less at
the density level than the pointwise number suggests, so any claim that the \emph{law} was
recovered should be stated on the law. The figures, and what the scalar summary does and does not
mean for a conditional kernel, are in Appendix~\ref{app:hellinger}.

\noindent
The practical consequence is that no convergence test can be trusted to select the reported model:
$\corr$ settles at a median of $\convrhomed$ updates against $\convshapemed$ for the shape, with a
tail to $\convshapemax$, so a $\corr$-trace --- or a flat likelihood --- looks textbook-converged
over a kernel nowhere near converged in the coordinate every non-Gaussian claim depends on. Every reported estimator is therefore selected on
a disjoint validation bundle, at every rung (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}). The fitted density,
the settling iterations and the clean-against-channel comparison are Appendix~\ref{app:rates}.


\section{What the structure buys}
\label{sec:networks}

The three levels above establish what is recoverable. This section asks what the structural
assumption is worth, and is deliberately the shortest in the note. As stated in the abstract the
comparison is asymmetric by construction, so what follows measures the value of that structure and
says nothing about neural denoisers in general. The baseline in Table~\ref{tab:pointwise} is a
fully connected network trained by denoising score matching in both standard parameterisations,
scored against the deterministic grid-BP reference on held-out sequences --- carrying neither
weight sharing nor a locality prior. Below we replace it with one that carries both.

\paragraph{How much of this is the architecture?} A ratio against a fully connected network cannot

cheaper explanation. Measured at the headline protocol --- every site scored, both arms selected

against a weight-shared window head the advantage is $\structratiolo$--$\structratiohi\times$,

convolutional stack and a bidirectional message-passing network, both able in principle to
propagate across the whole chain, neither of which beat it: on this family, at these sizes, the strongest
amortised-inference baseline tested is the simplest one screened. So the scoped statement is:
\emph{on this family, at these sizes, and with each arm's budget chosen on held-out data, the
structured estimator attains lower pointwise denoising error at equal training-set size than the
networks we trained --- by roughly an order of magnitude against a generic function approximator,
and by $\structratiolo$--$\structratiohi\times$ against a baseline that shares weights across
sites.}

\section{Limitations and outlook}
\label{sec:limits}

The model assumes exact first-order Markov structure, and under a non-Markov prior the estimator is
misspecified in a way more data does not repair: a global latent adds a rank-one term a chain
absorbs, while long-range precision coupling is not low-rank. Seven further scope conditions are in
Appendix~\ref{app:limits}. Left open: whether the pointwise advantage survives into generation
once both arms are selected on convergence rather than a fixed count; whether mixture capacity
helps an estimator whose grid resolves its narrowest component; and which architectures exploit
Markovianity, at what depth \citep{mei2024unet,garnierbrun2024transformers}.

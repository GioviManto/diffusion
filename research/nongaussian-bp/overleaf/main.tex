\begin{abstract}
Score-based generative models are trained by denoising regression, and the function they
approximate --- the posterior mean of the clean signal given its noised version --- is
unavailable in closed form for essentially every distribution of interest, so the error a network
makes cannot be separated from the difficulty of the object it approximates. We study a setting
where that separation is possible: a first-order Markov chain on $\Reals^{\nsites}$ with
continuous, non-Gaussian transitions. Coordinatewise Ornstein--Uhlenbeck noising multiplies the
prior by unary factors, so the posterior remains a chain and sum-product on it is exact at the
level of functional messages; a truncated grid turns this into a finite-state model for which the
recursion is exact up to floating point, with truncation and quadrature errors measured
separately. We then drop the assumption that the transition kernel is known and estimate it from
noised sequences alone, within the linear-autoregressive family
$\kernelth(a'\mid a) = \innov_\params(a' - \corr a)$ with both $\corr$ and the innovation density
free: the expectation step is exact for the discretised model and Fisher's identity supplies the
gradient without differentiating the recursion. Under the correctly specified Markov model and at
the budgets tested, the estimator attains lower pointwise denoising error than the neural
baselines we trained, by a margin that widens with data. Through reverse diffusion the pointwise
ranking does not carry over: two parameterisations of the same network, trained on identical data
and differing only in whether they regress the noise or the clean signal, are ranked in opposite
orders by two standard pointwise metrics while the laws they generate differ by $2.6$ standard
errors, and the validation rule one would naturally use selects the arm that misses the target by
$8\sigma$. We also record a correction: an earlier version of that comparison stopped EM at $40$
iterations, at which the innovation shape is far from converged although the autoregressive
coefficient appears settled, and both the dissociation reported there and its attribution to
model capacity fail once the kernel is fitted to convergence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Generative diffusion produces data $a\in\Reals^{\nsites}$ from a distribution $\Pz$ known only
through samples, by noising and then reversing \citep{sohl2015,ho2020,song2021}. Reversal is
driven by the score $\score(x,t)=\nabla_x\log\Pt(x)$, which is learned by regression. What that
regression targets is the posterior mean of the clean signal, and for realistic data it is
unknown, so a trained model cannot be scored against the thing it is approximating.

This note studies a family where the target is computable and not trivial. Distributions with
tractable population scores are scarce, which is why the analytical literature leans on
Gaussians and Gaussian mixtures; those are poor probes of structure, since the score is linear
and any question about how a method exploits correlation has a degenerate answer. Hierarchical
and discrete graphical models supply further tractable families
\citep{sclocchi2025phase,garnierbrun2024transformers,mei2024unet}, and the model here is a
continuous, non-Gaussian member of the same class.

A first-order Markov chain gives a nonlinear score that remains exactly computable, for a
structural reason: coordinatewise noising contributes one unary factor per site, which reweights
node potentials without creating edges among the latent variables, so the posterior factor graph
is the prior's chain.

We ask three questions. First, what does inference on the chain buy over a second-order Gaussian
approximation, and where in the noise schedule does it matter. Second, whether the transition
kernel can be estimated from noised data alone, and at what cost. Third, how the resulting
estimator compares with trained denoisers, both pointwise and after reverse diffusion.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item A denoising reference for a continuous non-Gaussian correlated model
      (\S\ref{sec:bp}), with truncation and quadrature error separated and measured
      (\S\ref{sec:numerics}), and the discretisation validated against a closed form and against
      brute-force enumeration.
\item An identification of what single-Gaussian message closure computes on this model: exactly
      the LMMSE estimator of the covariance-matched Gaussian model
      (Proposition~\ref{prop:lmmse}), proved directly, since a degree-two chain admits no
      large-degree central-limit argument.
\item Estimation of the kernel --- autoregressive coefficient and innovation density together
      --- from noised sequences, with an expectation step that is exact for the discretised
      model and no differentiation through the recursion (\S\ref{sec:em}).
\item A pointwise comparison against neural baselines (\S\ref{sec:pointwise}) and a generative
      comparison (\S\ref{sec:generation}) showing that the pointwise ranking does not order the
      generative one --- demonstrated within a single baseline, whose two standard
      parameterisations are ranked oppositely by two pointwise metrics and generate laws
      $2.6\sigma$ apart --- with a Gaussian negative control in which the spread collapses by a
      factor of $6.7$.
\item A correction to our own earlier analysis, reported rather than quietly fixed: stopping EM
      at $40$ iterations leaves the innovation shape far from converged while the autoregressive
      coefficient looks settled, which manufactured both a pointwise/generative dissociation and
      an apparent capacity effect of which ${\sim}96\%$ was convergence rate
      (\S\ref{sec:generation}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Model and the score--posterior identity}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Notation.} $\nsites$ is the chain length, which is also the ambient dimension, and
$\nseq$ is the number of observed sequences. Much of the diffusion literature uses $n$ for the
sample count and $d$ for the dimension; we index by sites because every graphical-model
statement below is indexed by sites. $\kernel$ always denotes the Markov transition kernel and
$\ngrid$ always the number of quadrature points. A full symbol table is
Appendix~\ref{app:notation}.

\paragraph{Forward process.} With $\gwn$ standardised Gaussian white noise,
$\langle\gwn(t)\gwn(t')\rangle=\delta(t-t')$, the forward Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process and its
solution are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:forward}
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} \;=\; -x + \sqrt{2}\,\gwn(t),
\qquad
x(t) \;=\; e^{-t} a + \sqrt{\Dt}\, z,
\qquad
\Dt = 1 - e^{-2t},
\quad z\sim\normal{0}{I}.
\end{equation}
The reverse-time convention, including the sign and the diffusion coefficient, is fixed once in
Appendix~\ref{app:notation} and used without variation.

\paragraph{Prior.} $\Pz$ factorises as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:prior}
\Pz(a) \;=\; \mu(a_1)\prod_{i=2}^{\nsites} \kernel(a_i \mid a_{i-1}),
\qquad
\kernel(a'\mid a) \;=\; \innov(a' - \corr a),
\end{equation}
with $\innov$ an innovation density of mean zero and variance $\ivar$, and $|\corr|<1$. The
implementation draws $a_1\sim\normal{0}{1}$ and sets $\ivar = 1-\corr^2$.

\begin{remark}[Covariance-stationary, not strictly stationary]
\label{rem:stationary}
With $\ivar = 1-\corr^2$ and $\Var(a_1)=1$, second moments propagate exactly: $\Var(a_i)=1$ for
every $i$ and $\Cov(a_i,a_j)=\corr^{|i-j|}$, whatever the shape of $\innov$. The chain is
therefore \emph{covariance-stationary}. It is \textbf{not} strictly stationary in distribution
for non-Gaussian $\innov$: the invariant law of $a_i=\corr a_{i-1}+\varepsilon_i$ is the law of
$\sum_{k\ge0}\corr^k\varepsilon_{-k}$, which is not Gaussian, so drawing $a_1$ Gaussian starts
the chain off the invariant law and the marginal of $a_i$ drifts towards it over a burn-in of
order $1/\log(1/\corr)\approx 6$ sites at $\corr=0.85$. This does not affect the comparisons
below, because every family shares the same $a_1$ law and the same covariance and so still
differs only beyond second moments; but $\ivar=1-\corr^2$ alone does not deliver strict
stationarity, and we do not claim it does.
\end{remark}

Variance matching is what makes the family a controlled probe: Gaussian, Laplace, Student,
uniform and bimodal innovations share $\Cov(a_i,a_j)=\corr^{|i-j|}$ exactly and differ only in
higher moments.

\paragraph{The score is a posterior mean.} From \eqref{eq:forward}, $\Pt$ is a Gaussian
convolution of $\Pz$. Differentiating under the integral and dividing by $\Pt(x)$ --- the
Gaussian factor over $\Pt(x)$ is exactly the posterior density $P(a\mid x,t)$ --- gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score}
\score(x,t) \;=\; -\,\frac{x - e^{-t}\,\pmean{a}}{\Dt},
\qquad
\pmean{a} = \int \mathrm{d}a\; a\, P(a\mid x,t).
\end{equation}
Differentiation under the integral is justified whenever $\Pz$ has a finite first moment
(Appendix~\ref{app:identity}). Equation~\eqref{eq:score} is a change of variables, not an
approximation: computing the score and computing the posterior mean are the same problem. The
identity is classical in the empirical-Bayes literature
\citep{robbins1956,miyasawa1961,efron2011} and underlies denoising score matching
\citep{vincent2011,hyvarinen2005}. For squared loss, $\pmean{a}$ minimises the conditional risk
(Appendix~\ref{app:identity}), so it is the reference every denoiser below is scored against.

\section{Inference on the noised chain}
\label{sec:bp}

Conditioning \eqref{eq:prior} on $x$ gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:posterior}
P(a \mid x,t) \;\propto\;
\mu(a_1)\prod_{i=2}^{\nsites} \kernel(a_i\mid a_{i-1})
\prod_{i=1}^{\nsites} \like(x_i \mid a_i),
\qquad
\like(x_i\mid a_i) \propto \exp\!\left[-\frac{(x_i - e^{-t}a_i)^2}{2\Dt}\right].
\end{equation}
The likelihood factors are unary: the forward noise is independent across coordinates, so each
observation touches one latent variable. They reweight node potentials and add no edges, and the
posterior factor graph is the prior's chain (Figure~\ref{fig:factorgraph}). A chain is a tree,
so sum-product returns the exact marginals \citep{pearl1988,kschischang2001,mezard2009}: with
\begin{align}
\label{eq:messages}
\lmsg_{i}(a_i) &\propto \int \mathrm{d}a_{i-1}\;
\kernel(a_i\mid a_{i-1})\,\like(x_{i-1}\mid a_{i-1})\,\lmsg_{i-1}(a_{i-1}), \nonumber\\[-2pt]
\rmsg_{i}(a_i) &\propto \int \mathrm{d}a_{i+1}\;
\kernel(a_{i+1}\mid a_i)\,\like(x_{i+1}\mid a_{i+1})\,\rmsg_{i+1}(a_{i+1}),
\end{align}
initialised by $\lmsg_1 = \mu$ and $\rmsg_{\nsites}\equiv 1$, the single-site marginal is
$P(a_i\mid x,t) \propto \lmsg_i(a_i)\like(x_i\mid a_i)\rmsg_i(a_i)$ and the pairwise marginal on
edge $(i-1,i)$ is $\propto \lmsg_{i-1}\like(x_{i-1}\mid a_{i-1})\kernel(a_i\mid
a_{i-1})\like(x_i\mid a_i)\rmsg_i$. The local likelihood is absorbed \emph{before} propagation,
so $\like(x_i\mid a_i)$ appears exactly once in each marginal; this convention is fixed
throughout and pinned by a test. These are the forward--backward recursions of hidden Markov
models \citep{baum1970,rabiner1989} written for a continuous state; when everything is Gaussian
they reduce to the Kalman filter and RTS smoother \citep{kalman1960,rauch1965}.

\begin{remark}[Four statements, kept apart]
\label{rem:exact}
(i) Sum-product on \eqref{eq:posterior} is \emph{exact at the level of functional messages}, as a
consequence of the graph being a tree. (ii) Messages are functions on $\Reals$, so an
implementation must represent them; a truncated uniform grid $[-\halfwidth,\halfwidth]$ with
$\ngrid$ points, spacing $\gridh$ and composite trapezoidal weights defines a \emph{finite-state
model}, for which the recursion is \emph{exact up to floating-point arithmetic}. (iii) That
finite-state model \emph{approximates} the continuous one, with an error that must be measured.
(iv) Any reported statistic of generated samples is in addition a \emph{Monte Carlo estimate}.
We write ``exact tree inference'' for (i), ``exact for the discretised model'' for (ii), and
``grid-BP reference'' for the numerical arm; we never abbreviate any of these to ``exact''. In
particular, the posterior marginals are not numerically exact merely because the graph is a tree.
\end{remark}

Naive marginalisation of \eqref{eq:posterior} on a grid costs $O(\ngrid^{\nsites-1})$; the
recursion costs $O(\nsites\ngrid^2)$ per sequence, each message update being one
$\ngrid\times\ngrid$ matrix--vector product. This is the sense in which continuous BP is
computationally operational: the messages are functions, and a finite representation of them
turns the recursion into dense linear algebra whose cost is quadratic in the representation size
and linear in the chain length.

\section{Numerical control and the Gaussian second-order baseline}
\label{sec:numerics}

\subsection{Separating truncation from quadrature}

Two error sources are separated by construction. \emph{Truncation}: mass outside
$[-\halfwidth,\halfwidth]$ is discarded rather than transported. \emph{Quadrature}: the
trapezoidal rule is second order in $\gridh$ for integrands with bounded second derivative;
$\innov(e)\propto e^{-|e|/b}$ has a discontinuous derivative at the origin, so we claim no
better than second order for the Laplace family and no spectral accuracy for it.

Figure~\ref{fig:grid} measures both. Panel (c) reports the edge-cell mass of the forward
messages, taken as the worst site within a chain and then summarised over $256$ chains per
configuration. This is an \emph{empirical diagnostic} of how much mass approaches the truncation
boundary, not a bound on the truncation error, and the distinction is not pedantic: a single
sampled trajectory --- which is what an earlier version of this diagnostic reported --- lands
near the \emph{median} over chains, while the maximum is some two orders of magnitude higher. We
therefore plot both the maximum and the ninetieth percentile. The quantity that carries the
argument is panel (d) together with the column-mass residuals, which involve no sample at all.
Truncation responds to the domain and not the resolution: the residual falls from
$3\times10^{-4}$ at $\halfwidth=4$ to $1.4\times10^{-8}$ at $\halfwidth=8$ and
$1.4\times10^{-10}$ at $\halfwidth=10$, essentially independently of $\ngrid$.
Panel (d) reports the column-normalisation residual of the transition matrix restricted to
interior columns, where no tail is truncated: at $\halfwidth=8$ it is
$3.8\times10^{-3},\,9.6\times10^{-4},\,2.4\times10^{-4}$ at $\gridh = 0.08, 0.04, 0.02$, a
factor of four per halving, which is the predicted second order. The two diagnostics are
therefore doing different jobs, and quoting one convergence sweep alone would not have
established the other. The working configuration $\ngrid=401$, $\halfwidth=8$ is chosen from
these curves.

\subsection{Validation where the answer is known}

Take $\innov=\normal{0}{\ivar}$. Then $a$ is jointly Gaussian with
$(\Sigma_0)_{ij}=\corr^{|i-j|}$, and so is $x$, with $\Sigma_t = e^{-2t}\Sigma_0 + \Dt I$, giving
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gaussian-closed}
\pmean{a} = e^{-t}\Sigma_0\Sigma_t^{-1}x,
\qquad
\score(x,t) = -\Sigma_t^{-1}x .
\end{equation}
The clean precision $\Sigma_0^{-1}$ is tridiagonal, and the posterior precision
$(e^{-2t}/\Dt)I + \Sigma_0^{-1}$ stays tridiagonal at every $t$: being Markov is a statement
about the precision, not the covariance, which is dense. The Gaussian case fixes intuition ---
the score is a fixed linear map, so denoising is linear regression, which is exactly why a
Gaussian prior is a poor probe of structure --- and it supplies two independent checks.

At $\ngrid=401$, $\halfwidth=8$ the discretised recursion reproduces \eqref{eq:gaussian-closed}
to $9.2\times10^{-15}$ relative, and agrees with brute-force enumeration of the discretised
posterior --- summing over all $\ngrid^{\nsites}$ configurations on a short chain, sharing no code
with the message passing --- to $1.6\times10^{-14}$ on posterior means, $1.8\times10^{-15}$ on the
log-evidence and $3.9\times10^{-16}$ on the pairwise statistic. Neither check involves
non-Gaussian machinery: the pipeline is verified where the answer is known before being used where
it is not. On the Laplace chain an independent importance-sampling estimate agrees with the
recursion on the evidence to within five Monte Carlo standard errors at $10^6$ samples and on the
posterior mean to $10^{-2}$ relative at $2\times10^6$; these are test assertions whose tolerances
are set by Monte Carlo error, not by the grid (\codefile{tests/test\_em\_bp.py}).

\subsection{The Gaussian second-order baseline}
\label{sec:closure}

A standard approximation propagates only the first two moments of each message. Under
$a_i = \corr a_{i-1} + \varepsilon_i$, a message with mean $m$ and variance $v$ transforms as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:closure}
\text{forward:}\quad m \mapsto \corr m,\;\; v \mapsto \corr^2 v + \ivar ;
\qquad
\text{backward:}\quad m \mapsto m/\corr,\;\; v \mapsto (v+\ivar)/\corr^2 ,
\end{equation}
the backward map being the forward one inverted because that recursion integrates over the
output variable (it requires $\corr\neq0$), and a Gaussian message times a Gaussian likelihood is
Gaussian. The propagation uses only the mean and variance of $\innov$.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:lmmse}
For the chain \eqref{eq:prior} with linear transitions, Gaussian unary likelihoods and
$\E[a]=0$, moment-projected sum-product returns the LMMSE estimator of the covariance-matched
Gaussian model,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lmmse}
\lmmse
= \Cov(a,x)\,\Cov(x)^{-1} x
= e^{-t}\Sigma_0\left(e^{-2t}\Sigma_0 + \Dt I\right)^{-1} x ,
\end{equation}
for every innovation law with mean zero and variance $\ivar$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Both maps in \eqref{eq:closure} depend on $\innov$ only through its mean and variance, so the
recursion returns the same function of $x$ for every innovation law with moments $(0,\ivar)$ ---
in particular the same as on the covariance-matched \emph{Gaussian} chain. On that chain every
message is exactly Gaussian, so the moment projection is the identity and the recursion returns
the posterior mean. For jointly Gaussian zero-mean $(a,x)$ the posterior mean is linear in $x$
and therefore coincides with the best linear estimator under squared loss, which is
\eqref{eq:lmmse}. A full derivation is Appendix~\ref{app:lmmse}.
\end{proof}

We call \eqref{eq:lmmse} the \emph{Gaussian second-order baseline} and use that name throughout.
On this model, message closure, moment projection and outright replacement of the prior by its
covariance-matched Gaussian all coincide; they separate for richer message families, and the
name is chosen not to prejudge which mechanism is at work. We do not call the method AMP:
a degree-two chain supplies no large-degree central-limit justification for that terminology, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:lmmse} is proved directly instead of argued asymptotically. The zero-mean
hypothesis is load-bearing: with $\E[a]=1.3$ the estimator \eqref{eq:lmmse} departs from the true
LMMSE by $0.65$ in maximum absolute value, because the general form carries
$\E[a] + \Cov(a,x)\Cov(x)^{-1}(x-\E[x])$.

The gap between \eqref{eq:lmmse} and grid BP locates where non-Gaussianity matters
(Figure~\ref{fig:closure}). On a Laplace chain at $\corr=0.85$ the median relative score error
falls from $0.175$ at $t=0.08$ to $7.6\times10^{-5}$ at $t=2.4$; large $t$ Gaussianises the
posterior, so the shape of $\innov$ survives only where the likelihood is weak. The same ordering
holds across the other innovation families.

\section{Estimating the transition kernel from noised sequences}
\label{sec:em}

We now treat $\kernel$ as unknown and observe only noised sequences $x^\mu$,
$\mu=1,\dots,\nseq$, possibly at several noise levels. Write $\params$ for the kernel parameters
and $\Lik(\params)=\sum_\mu \log P_{t,\params}(x^\mu)$.

\paragraph{What is and is not estimated.} The kernel is estimated within the
linear-autoregressive family
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kernel}
\kernelth(a'\mid a) \;=\; \sum_{c=1}^{\ncomp} \pi_c \,
\normal{a' - \corr a - \nu_c}{s_c^2},
\end{equation}
in which \textbf{both} the autoregressive coefficient $\corr$ and the innovation density are
free. At $\ncomp=4$ the free-parameter count is $\corr$ (one), weights (four, one simplex
constraint, so three), locations (four) and scales (four): \textbf{twelve}, against $3\ncomp$ in
general. Every experiment initialises $\corr$ at $0.3$ against a truth of $0.85$, so the
autoregressive coefficient is genuinely recovered rather than supplied; from initialisations at
$\corr^{(0)}\in\{0,\,0.3,\,0.6,\,-0.4\}$ the fitted value lands in $[0.8497, 0.8507]$
(Figure~\ref{fig:em}). What is \emph{not} estimated is the initial law $\mu(a_1)$: it is held
fixed at $\normal{0}{1}$, which is exactly the law the data-generating process uses, so there is
no misspecification in $\mu$ anywhere in these results --- but neither is there evidence that it
could be recovered. The linear form of \eqref{eq:kernel} is likewise assumed, not learned: what
is free is a scalar and a density, not an arbitrary bivariate transition.

\paragraph{Expectation step.} Many latent-variable models need an approximate E-step; this one
does not, because the posterior is a tree and sum-product returns exact single-site \emph{and
pairwise} marginals --- exactly for the discretised model, in the sense of
Remark~\ref{rem:exact}(ii). The complete-data log-likelihood is a sum over edges, so the
$\params$-dependent part of the auxiliary function $\Qfun(\params\mid\params^{(r)})$ depends on
the data only through
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:xi}
\Xis_{kl} \;=\; \sum_{\mu=1}^{\nseq}\sum_{i=2}^{\nsites}
P\!\left(a_{i-1}=\gridpt{k},\; a_i=\gridpt{l} \;\middle|\; x^\mu\right),
\end{equation}
the expected transition mass between grid points, the continuum analogue of the expected
transition counts of Baum--Welch \citep{baum1970}. Sufficiency requires a homogeneous kernel
shared across sites and sequences, a fixed grid, an initial law excluded from $\params$, and
$\params$-free likelihood factors --- the last being what licenses observations at several noise
levels to add into one $\Xis$. Given those, the maximisation step never revisits the data and
costs $O(P\ngrid^2)$ for $P$ parameters, independently of $\nseq$. Forming $\Xis$ costs
$O(\nseq\nsites\ngrid^2)$ per iteration and $O(\ngrid^2)$ storage, so ``one matrix'' is a
statement about sufficiency, not about inference being cheap.

\paragraph{Gradient without differentiating the recursion.} Fisher's identity
\citep{fisher1925,louis1982,cappe2005} gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fisher}
\nabla_\params \Lik(\params)
= \sum_{\mu}\E_\params\!\left[\nabla_\params \log P_\params(a,x^\mu)\,\middle|\,x^\mu\right]
= \big\langle \Xis(\params),\; \nabla_\params \log \kernelth \big\rangle ,
\end{equation}
the exact gradient of the \emph{marginal} log-likelihood from one sum-product pass. Sum-product
is not a computation graph to backpropagate through; it supplies the conditional expectation. We
verify \eqref{eq:fisher} against finite differences of the discretised evidence to
$\sim10^{-9}$.

\paragraph{Which algorithm actually runs.} Equation~\eqref{eq:fisher} licenses gradient ascent on
$\Lik$; EM instead increases $\Qfun$. Both are implemented and neither dominates: on the smooth
Gaussian kernel gradient ascent reaches EM's optimum to $2\times10^{-9}$ nats while needing a
tuned step size, and on the Laplace kernel it attains a higher likelihood, because there the
exact maximiser of $\Qfun$ is a lattice artefact of the discretisation. The results below use EM.
For the mixture kernel \eqref{eq:kernel} the M-step is itself iterative --- the complete-data
problem carries a second latent variable, the mixture label, and we run four inner
conditional-maximisation sweeps --- so the algorithm is \textbf{generalised EM} of ECM type
\citep{dempster1977,meng1993}: each step increases $\Qfun$ without exactly maximising it, which
preserves the monotone-ascent guarantee \citep{wu1983} but not the exact-maximiser property. We
do not call it exact EM. Monotonicity is also observed: the largest decrease of $\Lik$ across
iterations is $0$ in all eight runs of Figure~\ref{fig:em}. Initialisation, stopping rule and
parameter constraints are in Appendix~\ref{app:learning}.

\begin{remark}[Stop on the shape, not on $\corr$]
\label{rem:stopping}
The coordinates of $\params$ do not converge at the same rate, and the gap is large enough to
mislead. At $\corr=0.85$, $\nseq=512$, $\ncomp=8$ the autoregressive coefficient is flat from
iteration ${\sim}25$, while the fitted innovation excess kurtosis reads $0.84$ at $30$ iterations,
$1.85$ at $60$, $2.30$ at $120$ and $2.29$ at $400$. Monitoring $\corr$ --- the natural thing to
plot, and a quantity whose trace looks textbook-converged --- certifies a kernel that still
carries a third of the true higher-order structure.

This is the same asymmetry the information analysis predicts: shape is the fragile coordinate
under the channel, and it is also the slow one under the optimiser. Any stopping rule for this
model should therefore be read off a shape statistic. Every estimation result in this paper uses
$120$ iterations, past the plateau above; \S\ref{sec:generation} reports what happened in an
earlier version that used $40$.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Innovation centering.} The fitted mixture is \emph{not} constrained to satisfy
$\sum_c\pi_c\nu_c=0$. Imposing it by reprojection after each update is not a maximisation step
and broke monotone ascent by $\sim10^{-2}$ nats in testing, so the condition is monitored as a
diagnostic: it lands at $-4.6\times10^{-3}$ in the runs of Figure~\ref{fig:em}. The fitted family
therefore does not satisfy the hypothesis of Proposition~\ref{prop:lmmse}, which applies to the
true kernel and only approximately to the estimated one.

\begin{assumption}[Identifiability, assumed and not proved]
\label{ass:ident}
Gaussian convolution at finite $t$ is injective on distributions --- the characteristic function
acquires a factor $e^{-\Dt\|\xi\|^2/2}$, which has no zeros, and $e^{-t}>0$ --- so $\Pt$
determines $\Pz$. \emph{Kernel} identifiability does not follow. It additionally requires: the
initial law fixed or separately identifiable; a full-support $\mu$, since $\kernel$ is pinned
down only on the support of $\mu$; the family \eqref{eq:kernel} identifiable as a parametric
family, which for finite mixtures holds on the interior of the parameter set --- all $\pi_c>0$
and the pairs $(\nu_c,s_c)$ distinct \citep{teicher1963,yakowitz1968} --- and is exactly where an
over-specified $\ncomp$ need not stay; and identification modulo label permutation. We assume
these rather than prove them.
\end{assumption}

\paragraph{What noising destroys.} The channel does not degrade every coordinate of the kernel
equally, and the disparity is large enough to govern what can be learned at all. Measuring it
requires a parameter that is genuinely \emph{higher order}: in the Gaussian model with parameters
$(\rho,\ivar)$ both coordinates are second order, since $\ivar$ is a scale, so a comparison
between them says nothing about shape. We therefore use a generalised-Gaussian innovation
\[
  \varphi_\beta(e)\;\propto\;\exp\!\big(-(|e|/s)^{\beta}\big),
  \qquad s(\ivar,\beta)=\sqrt{\ivar\,\Gamma(1/\beta)/\Gamma(3/\beta)},
\]
in which $\beta$ moves the fourth moment while holding the variance at $\ivar$ exactly:
$\beta=1$ is Laplace (excess kurtosis $3$), $\beta=2$ is Gaussian (excess kurtosis $0$).

Comparing raw diagonal entries of the information matrix would ignore the coupling between
coordinates, so we report the \emph{efficient} information for each, obtained by projecting out
the other two,
$\mathcal I_{\beta\beta\cdot\eta}=J_{\beta\beta}-J_{\beta\eta}J_{\eta\eta}^{-1}J_{\eta\beta}$
with $\eta=(\rho,\ivar)$, which is what bounds any estimator of $\beta$ that must also fit the
nuisance parameters. Between $t=0.05$ and $t=1.6$ the efficient information falls by $56$--$87$
for the correlation, $235$--$522$ for the variance, and $8.7\times10^{3}$--$6.8\times10^{4}$ for
the shape, so shape information decays $112$ to $1222$ times faster than correlation information
depending on the true $\beta$
(\codefile{outputs/exp\_22/shape\_information.csv}). The nuisance correction is itself
asymmetric: at $t=1.6$ the correlation and variance coordinates become $90\%$ correlated and each
loses a factor of about $5.5$ to coupling, while $\beta$ is nearly orthogonal to both and loses
almost nothing. Higher-order structure of $\innov$ is thus far more fragile under the channel
than second-order structure --- by two to three orders of magnitude, not the factor of five a
comparison within the Gaussian model would suggest --- a fact \S\ref{sec:generation} returns to.

\paragraph{What the channel costs in practice, against the no-noising baseline.} The information
numbers above are asymptotic and per noise level. What an estimator actually loses also depends
on the protocol, so we measure it directly against the case where the question does not arise: if
the clean chain is available, identifying $\kernel$ needs no noising at all --- it is plain
maximum likelihood, \codefile{em.fit\_clean}, and the denoiser at every $t$ then follows from BP
for free. Both arms receive the \emph{same} $\nseq$ clean chains and the same iteration count;
the only difference is that one of them never sees them clean. Three replicates, paired
(\codefile{outputs/exp\_06\_em\_parameter\_recovery/clean\_vs\_noised\_*.csv}):

unresolved rather than as a null result: three replicates give a standard error of $0.15$ on an
effect of $0.22$, and the per-budget signs are not consistent.

\section{Experimental protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Unless stated otherwise: Laplace chain, $\nsites=32$, $\corr=0.85$, grid $\ngrid=401$,
$\halfwidth=8$, five training noise levels $t\in\{0.1,0.2,0.4,0.8,1.6\}$, and $256$ held-out
sequences drawn from a seed disjoint from every training seed. Estimators are scored against
grid BP under the \emph{true} kernel on those held-out sequences.

Neural baselines are trained by denoising score matching in both standard parameterisations
(predicting the noise, and predicting the clean signal). The fully connected network has $25{,}248$
parameters; the locality-respecting baseline is a weight-shared one-dimensional convolution.
Six replicates vary both the training set and the initialisation --- the replicate index is mixed
into every training seed --- while the test set and its reference are common to all six, so the
reported intervals cover sampling and optimisation variance together. Architectures, parameter
counts, optimiser, step counts, batch sizes, seeds and hardware are in
Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}; the reverse-integration protocol is in Appendix~\ref{app:reverse}.

\begin{remark}[Information asymmetry]
\label{rem:asymmetry}
The estimator is given the Markov factorisation, the linear-autoregressive form of the kernel,
and homogeneity across sites. The networks are given none of these. Every comparison below is
therefore between a correctly specified structured model and generic function approximators, and
should be read as measuring the value of that structure, not as a claim about neural denoisers in
general.
\end{remark}

\section{Reverse generation}
\label{sec:generation}

Denoising accuracy is not what a generative model is judged on. Errors enter the reverse
integration at every step and accumulate unevenly across the schedule, so a pointwise ranking
need not survive. We integrate the reverse process of Appendix~\ref{app:reverse} under each
estimator with common random numbers across estimators.

\paragraph{Behaviour near $t=0$.} Integration stops at $\tmin>0$, and the reason is
\emph{conditioning} rather than a generic divergence of the score. Equation~\eqref{eq:score}
carries $1/\Dt$, but its numerator vanishes at the same order: for a $\Pz$ with a $C^1$ density
$\score(x,t)\to\nabla\log\Pz(x)$, which is finite. Four effects are at work --- a genuine
singularity for the compactly supported uniform-innovation chain only, numerical cancellation,
a likelihood factor the grid stops resolving, and a reverse drift whose Jacobian scales like
$1/\Dt$ --- and they are separated in Appendix~\ref{app:reverse}. The conclusion, $\tmin>0$, is
unchanged; the reason for it is not that $\score$ must blow up.

\paragraph{Comparing like with like.} What each estimator produces is a sample of $P_{\tmin}$,
not of $\Pz$, so comparing it against clean data measures the stopping point rather than the
score: independent Gaussian noise of variance $v$ on an innovation of variance $\ivar$ multiplies
the excess kurtosis by $(\ivar/(\ivar+v))^2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:reverse}). We instead draw a
reference from $\Pz$ and noise it forward to the same $\tmin$, so both sides are samples of the
same law and the target follows in closed form. Run with the true kernel the sampler reproduces
it to within $1.4\%$ across budgets (medians $1.897$, $1.883$, $1.914$, $1.908$ at
$\nseq=32,128,512,2048$ against $1.9098$), which validates the integrator and the statistics
before any estimator is compared.

\paragraph{Sampler convergence.} Over an eightfold range of step counts the reference arm's
generated kurtosis is $1.989\pm0.127$, $1.817\pm0.096$, $1.913\pm0.108$, $1.936\pm0.099$ at
$100$, $200$, $400$, $800$ steps: all within one standard error of the target, with the arm
ordering unchanged throughout (Figure~\ref{fig:steps}, Appendix~\ref{app:extra}). The qualitative comparison is therefore
not an artefact of one discretisation over that range. Two further step counts, $1600$ and
$3200$, were launched but produced no output, so the sweep covers $100$--$800$ and we claim
nothing beyond it.

\paragraph{The pointwise ranking does not order the generative one.} The sharpest form of this is
not a contest between the estimator and the networks. It is that \emph{the same network}, trained
on the same chains for the same number of steps, generates two materially different laws
depending on a choice usually treated as an implementation detail: whether it regresses the noise
($\epsilon$) or the clean signal ($x_0$). At $\nseq=512$, over six seeds
(\codefile{simple/results.csv}):

\begin{remark}[These residuals are not innovations]
\label{rem:residuals}
The statistic is the AR-filtered residual $r_i=x_i-\rho x_{i-1}$ of a sample of $P_{\tmin}$, not
an innovation. Substituting $x_i=\alpha_t a_i+\sqrt{\Delta_t}z_i$ and $a_i=\rho a_{i-1}+\innov_i$
gives $r_i=\alpha_t\innov_i+\sqrt{\Delta_t}\,(z_i-\rho z_{i-1})$, so adjacent residuals are
correlated,
\[
  \operatorname{Cov}(r_i,r_{i+1})=-\rho\Delta_t,
  \qquad
  \operatorname{Corr}(r_i,r_{i+1})=\frac{-\rho\Delta_t}{\alpha_t^{2}\ivar+\Delta_t(1+\rho^{2})},
\]
which is $-0.0997$ at $\rho=0.85$, $\tmin=0.02$ and grows to $-0.286$ at $\tmin=0.1$. The noised
process is not AR(1); even in the Gaussian case it is ARMA-type. The marginal shape comparison
above remains valid and remains the sharpest discriminator between families at matched
covariance, but it does not measure recovery of the clean transition innovation law, and we do
not claim that it does. On the clean chain the same filter does recover innovations
(measured $|\mathrm{ACF}_1|<0.02$).
\end{remark}

\paragraph{The Gaussian control.} On a Gaussian chain the fitted mixture family \emph{contains}
the truth, so a misspecification account predicts the spread between arms collapses while an
information-theoretic one does not --- the channel degrades correlation information on a Gaussian
chain as well. It collapses. Over the same six seeds at $\nseq=512$ the spread across the three
arms is $0.545$ on the Laplace chain and $0.082$ here, a factor of $6.7$, and the estimator lands
at $0.042$ against a target of $0.038$: $0.2\sigma$, essentially exact, having converged onto it
from $0.381$ at $\nseq=32$. The estimator misses precisely when its model class does not contain
the truth and hits when it does.

threaten the reading. And the four-family comparison at matched covariance
(Table~\ref{tab:families}, Appendix~\ref{app:extra}) was run under the superseded protocol

with the convolution at $1.367$. Enlarging the mixture from $\ncomp=4$ to $\ncomp=8$ buys $0.542$
in fitted kurtosis at 40 iterations and $0.022$ at 200: roughly $96\%$ of the apparent capacity
benefit is a difference in convergence \emph{rate} at a fixed iteration budget, not in what the
model class can represent. At convergence $\ncomp=4$ fits the innovation law as well as
$\ncomp=8$. The generative ordering reverses with it, which is why the estimator arm above is on
target rather than below the networks.

This evidence is one seed at $800$ generated chains and $200$ integration steps --- enough to
withdraw the capacity attribution, not enough to replace it with a quantitative alternative, and
the sweep should be rerun at convergence. Table~\ref{tab:components} and
Figure~\ref{fig:capacity} are retained as the record of the superseded analysis rather than as
support for any claim made here. Nothing in \S\ref{sec:em}, \S\ref{sec:pointwise} or the
sample-efficiency results is affected: those experiments run at $120$ iterations
(\codefile{exp\_06}, \codefile{exp\_07}, \codefile{exp\_12}), which the sweep above shows is past
the plateau.

\begin{remark}[Where capacity stops paying, on a paired design]
\label{rem:paired}
Separate medians over a handful of seeds cannot resolve the small differences at the top of this
range, because most of the variance is the training draw and it is shared between capacities. We
therefore fit every $\ncomp$ on the \emph{same} training set within a cell and score it on the
\emph{same} held-out bundle, so the contrast is within-dataset and its standard error is an order
of magnitude below the effect. Held-out denoising MSE against the Bayes denoiser, six paired
seeds, differences against $\ncomp=1$: at $M=512$ the gains are $+3.8\times10^{-5}$
($\ncomp=2$), $-1.41\times10^{-3}$ ($4$), $-1.74\times10^{-3}$ ($8$), $-1.76\times10^{-3}$
($16$), with standard errors $1.3\times10^{-5}$ to $1.1\times10^{-4}$
(\codefile{outputs/exp\_16/paired\_local/paired\_contrasts.csv}).

Three consequences. $\ncomp=8$ is the optimum: $\ncomp=16$ differs from it by less than one
standard error and at $M=128$ is marginally worse. A two-component mixture is \emph{not} an
improvement on a single Gaussian innovation, and at $M=512$ is significantly worse than one. And
none of this is a discretisation artefact --- the narrowest fitted component is at least $8.8$
grid spacings wide in all sixty cells, with the effective component count tracking the nominal
one, so the mixture is genuinely using its capacity and every component is resolved.
\end{remark}

This is consistent with a capacity
bottleneck at small $\ncomp$ and inconsistent with the information account being the binding
constraint there. It does not show that information is never binding --- the curve has not
saturated, and we have not measured how much further it can go.

\section{Limitations and open questions}
\label{sec:limits}

The model assumes exact first-order Markov structure. Under a non-Markov prior the estimator is
misspecified in a way more data does not repair, and how much that costs turns out to depend
sharply on \emph{which} assumption is violated --- so we measured it rather than leaving it as a
caveat.

Two mechanisms, both with an exact reference. A shared global latent,
$a=(y+\beta g)/\sqrt{1+\beta^{2}}$ with $g\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, contaminates the prior with a
rank-one term; weak long-range precision coupling, $Q=Q_{\mathrm{AR}(1)}+\gamma Q_{\mathrm{long}}$,
does not. For Gaussian innovations both references are linear algebra; for the Laplace chain plus
a global latent the reference is exact by conditioning on $g$ and marginalising it with
Gauss--Hermite, since the prior is Markov given $g$ (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}).

The estimator is remarkably robust to the rank-one violation and fails quickly under the other.
Against the convolution, the ratio of relative score errors stays above $2.08$ across the whole
global-latent sweep up to $\beta=1$, where half the marginal variance is a shared constant; with
Laplace innovations it stays above $1.45$. Under long-range coupling it inverts at
$\gamma\approx0.10$ and reaches $0.47$ at $\gamma=0.4$, where the fully connected network also
overtakes the estimator. This is what the operator analysis of
\S\ref{sec:numerics} predicts: a global latent makes the score residual exactly rank one, which a
chain absorbs, while long-range coupling is not low-rank and cannot be represented. The fitted
parameters corroborate it --- EM converges to the best-Markov (Chow--Liu) projection to within
$0.0009$--$0.0128$ across the $\beta$ sweep but departs from it by up to $0.101$ under $\gamma$,
exactly where the chain family stops being able to represent the truth. So the honest scope is:
the structural prior survives rank-one contamination essentially intact, regardless of innovation
shape, and should not be relied on under genuine long-range structure.

The transition family \eqref{eq:kernel} is
additionally misspecified at small $\ncomp$, which \S\ref{sec:generation} measures and shows is
relieved by enlarging $\ncomp$. The initial law is fixed rather than estimated, and the chain is
covariance-stationary rather than strictly stationary (Remark~\ref{rem:stationary}); no run yet
initialises from the invariant non-Gaussian law. Identifiability is assumed
(Assumption~\ref{ass:ident}), not proved. Sum-product is exact on the posterior chain but the
implementation is a discretisation whose error, for the Laplace family, is second order rather
than spectral.

Inference costs $O(\nsites\ngrid^2)$ per sequence, measured at $210$--$289$ times a network
forward pass; since reverse diffusion calls the denoiser at every step, this is the practical
weak point. Training cost grows linearly in $\nseq$ while the network's is fixed by its step
count, so the advantage is in data, not wall-clock. The low parameter count is bought with a
structural assumption, and the advantage narrows as the parametric dimension grows: on a
discrete-alphabet variant it falls from about $15$ at six estimated parameters to $3.6$--$6.1$
at $132$.

The measurement gaps that remain are narrower than they were. The capacity sweep now includes
marginal likelihood and runtime against $\ncomp$: held-out evidence saturates by $\ncomp\approx8$
and cost is flat in $\ncomp$, since the $O(\nseq\nsites\ngrid^{2})$ expectation step dominates and
only the maximisation step scales with capacity. Model selection is on a disjoint validation
bundle throughout, and the oracle it replaces was measured at $0.999\times$ and $0.996\times$
(Table~\ref{tab:pointwise}). The locality statement is now exact rather than a proxy: under
strictly stationary initialisation the windowed estimator \emph{is} $\E[a_i \mid x_{i-r:i+r}]$,
because a contiguous window of a Markov chain is a chain with the correct endpoint law. That
correction does not change the measured rates --- the ratio of the stationary to the original
protocol has median $1.004$ across ninety cells --- so the earlier numbers stand, on better
footing.

What is still missing. The likelihoods reported here are a \emph{composite} objective: each clean
chain is observed at several noise levels and the groups are treated as independent, so every
marginal is correctly specified but the effective sample size is the number of clean chains, not
that times the number of levels. Identifiability remains assumed. The neural comparison is
against the two architectures described --- a fully connected network and a weight-shared
convolution --- and does not license a claim about modern sequence denoisers, which is the
comparison a practitioner would want (Remark~\ref{rem:asymmetry}).

\section{Conclusion}

When a sequential distribution is known to have low-dimensional Markov structure, learning a
diffusion score reduces to latent-variable inference plus estimation of a compact transition
model. Sum-product on the noised chain supplies a non-Gaussian reference whose discretisation
error is separately measured, and estimating the kernel from noised data alone gives a denoiser
substantially more accurate at small sample sizes than the networks we trained, at the cost of a
stronger structural assumption and much more expensive inference. Reverse diffusion does not
reproduce that ranking at small innovation capacity, and enlarging the innovation model improves
both axes together. The methodological point is the more portable one: an estimator can be an
order of magnitude better at the task it was fitted for and still generate worse samples, so a
structured method has to be judged on the generated distribution and not on denoising error
alone.

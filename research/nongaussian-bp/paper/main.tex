\begin{abstract}
Score-based generative models are trained by denoising regression, and the function they
approximate --- the posterior mean of the clean signal given its noised version --- is
unavailable in closed form for essentially every distribution of interest. This makes it hard
to separate the error a network makes from the difficulty of the object it approximates. We
study a setting where the posterior mean is computable and non-trivial: a first-order Markov
chain on $\Reals^{\nsites}$ with continuous, non-Gaussian transitions. Coordinatewise
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck noising multiplies the prior by unary factors, so the posterior remains a
chain, and sum-product inference on it is exact at the level of functional messages. We use
this to obtain a controlled benchmark, and then remove the assumption that the transition
kernel is known: it is estimated from noised sequences alone by maximum marginal likelihood,
with an exact expectation step and a gradient supplied by Fisher's identity, so the inference
recursion is never differentiated through. On a Laplace chain the estimator attains a lower
denoising error than trained neural denoisers at every data budget we tried, by roughly an
order of magnitude against a fully connected network and a factor of four against a
locality-respecting one. We then compare the same estimators through reverse diffusion. At a fixed, deliberately
small innovation parametrisation the ranking does not carry over --- the estimator remains the
most accurate pointwise while a convolutional network reproduces the innovation law more
faithfully --- and this holds across four non-Gaussian families, three chain lengths, and is
unchanged by a fourfold grid refinement. Varying the parametrisation identifies the cause: the
generated tail is limited by the capacity of the innovation model rather than by the
information in noised data, and the estimator overtakes the network once the mixture is rich
enough. We report both the dissociation and its resolution, since the first is what a fixed
model class produces and the second is what fixes it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Generative diffusion generates data $a\in\Reals^{\nsites}$ from a distribution $\Pz$ known
only through samples, by noising and then reversing \citep{sohl2015,ho2020,song2021}. In the
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck convention we follow throughout \citep{mezard2025notes}, the forward
process is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:forward}
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} \;=\; -x + \sqrt{2}\,\gwn(t),
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
x(t) \;=\; e^{-t} a + \sqrt{\Dt}\, z(t),
\qquad
\Dt = 1 - e^{-2t},
\end{equation}
with $z$ standard Gaussian. Reversal is driven by the score
$\score(x,t) = \nabla_x \log \Pt(x)$, and the central quantity is the posterior mean
$\pmean{a}$ of the clean signal.

\paragraph{Three distinct score objects.}
Confusing them is the source of a common misstatement, so we fix them at the outset.

The \emph{population score} is $\nabla_x\log\Pt(x)$ for the true $\Pz$. The
\emph{empirical score} replaces $\Pz$ by the empirical measure
$\widehat{P}_0 = \nseq^{-1}\sum_\mu \delta_{a^\mu}$ of the training set; after noising this is
a Gaussian mixture centred on the training points. The \emph{learned score} is what a
network with finite capacity, finite data and a particular optimiser actually produces.

The population denoising objective $\E\|f(x,t)-a\|^2$ is minimised over all measurable $f$ by
the population posterior mean. The \emph{empirical} objective, which is what is computed in
practice, is minimised by the \emph{empirical} posterior mean. Since exact reversal under the
empirical score returns the empirical distribution, a model that attained the unrestricted
optimum of its own training objective would reproduce its training set. That trained models
generalise is therefore a statement about the restrictions --- architecture, capacity,
optimisation, regularisation --- and not about the objective
\citep{biroli2024dynamical,kamb2024analytic}. We do not claim this is a complete account of
neural generalisation; we note it because it motivates wanting settings where the population
score is available for comparison.

\paragraph{Why a Markov chain.}
Distributions with tractable population scores are scarce, which is why the analytical
literature leans on Gaussians and Gaussian mixtures. Those are poor probes of structure: the
score is linear, so any question about how a model exploits correlation has a degenerate
answer. Hierarchical and discrete graphical models supply further tractable families
\citep{sclocchi2025phase,garnierbrun2024transformers,mei2024unet}, and the model here is a
continuous, non-Gaussian member of the same class.

A first-order Markov chain gives a genuinely nonlinear score while remaining exactly
computable, for a structural reason: coordinatewise noising contributes one unary factor per
site, which reweights node potentials without creating edges between latent variables, so the
posterior factor graph is the same chain as the prior's.

\paragraph{Questions.} We ask: (i) what does exact inference on the chain buy over the
Gaussian approximation, and where in the noise schedule does it matter; (ii) can the
transition kernel be estimated from noised data alone, and at what cost; (iii) how does the
resulting estimator compare with trained denoisers, both pointwise and after reverse
diffusion.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item An exact denoising benchmark for a continuous non-Gaussian correlated model
      (\S\ref{sec:bp}), with the discretisation error separated from the model error and
      measured (\S\ref{sec:numerics}).
\item An identification of what the single-Gaussian message closure computes: exactly the
      LMMSE estimator of the covariance-matched Gaussian model (\S\ref{sec:closure}).
\item Estimation of the unknown kernel from noised sequences, with an exact expectation step
      and no differentiation through the recursion (\S\ref{sec:em}).
\item A denoising comparison against neural baselines (\S\ref{sec:pointwise}) and a
      generative comparison (\S\ref{sec:generation}) which do not agree, together with the
      information asymmetry that plausibly explains the disagreement.
\end{itemize}

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Notation.} We write $\nsites$ for the chain length, which is also the ambient
dimension, and $\nseq$ for the number of observed sequences. (The source we follow for
diffusion notation uses $n$ for the number of samples and $d$ for the dimension; we index by
the chain because every graphical-model statement below is indexed by sites.) Sequences are
$a = (a_1,\dots,a_{\nsites})$, and $\Pz$ factorises as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:prior}
\Pz(a) \;=\; \mu(a_1)\prod_{i=2}^{\nsites} \kernel(a_i \mid a_{i-1}),
\qquad
\kernel(a'\mid a) \;=\; \innov(a' - \corr a),
\end{equation}
with $\innov$ an innovation density of mean zero and variance $\ivar$, and
$|\corr| < 1$. Choosing $\ivar = 1-\corr^2$ makes the chain stationary with unit marginal
variance and $\Cov(a_i,a_j) = \corr^{|i-j|}$ \emph{independently of the shape of $\innov$}.
This is what makes the family a controlled probe: Gaussian, Laplace, Student, uniform and
bimodal innovations can be arranged to share a covariance exactly and differ only beyond
second moments.

\paragraph{The score and the posterior mean.}
From \eqref{eq:forward}, $\Pt$ is the Gaussian convolution
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pt}
\Pt(x) \;=\; \int \mathrm{d}a \; \Pz(a) \,
\frac{1}{(2\pi\Dt)^{\nsites/2}}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{\|x - e^{-t}a\|^2}{2\Dt}\right].
\end{equation}
Differentiating under the integral,
\[
\nabla_x \Pt(x)
= \int \mathrm{d}a\; \Pz(a)\,
\frac{e^{-t}a - x}{\Dt}\,
\frac{e^{-\|x-e^{-t}a\|^2/2\Dt}}{(2\pi\Dt)^{\nsites/2}},
\]
and dividing by $\Pt(x)$, the Gaussian factor over $\Pt(x)$ is exactly the posterior density
$P(a\mid x,t)$, so
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score}
\score(x,t) \;=\; \nabla_x\log \Pt(x)
\;=\; -\,\frac{x - e^{-t}\,\pmean{a}}{\Dt},
\qquad
\pmean{a} = \int \mathrm{d}a\; a\, P(a\mid x,t).
\end{equation}
Differentiation under the integral is justified whenever $\Pz$ has a finite first moment, by
dominated convergence with the Gaussian kernel and its derivative. Equation~\eqref{eq:score}
is a change of variables, not an approximation: computing the score and computing the
posterior mean are the same problem.

\paragraph{The Bayes-optimal denoiser.} For squared loss and any $u$ that is a function of
$x$ alone,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:orthogonal}
\E\!\left[\|a-u\|^2 \,\middle|\, x\right]
=
\E\!\left[\|a - \pmean{a}\|^2 \,\middle|\, x\right] + \|u - \pmean{a}\|^2 ,
\end{equation}
so $\pmean{a}$ minimises the conditional risk, with an irreducible floor given by the first
term. Optimality is with respect to squared error specifically; a different loss selects a
different functional of the posterior.

\section{Exact inference on the noised chain}
\label{sec:bp}

Conditioning \eqref{eq:prior} on $x$ gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:posterior}
P(a \mid x,t) \;\propto\;
\mu(a_1)\prod_{i=2}^{\nsites} \kernel(a_i\mid a_{i-1})
\prod_{i=1}^{\nsites} \like(x_i \mid a_i),
\qquad
\like(x_i\mid a_i) \propto \exp\!\left[-\frac{(x_i - e^{-t}a_i)^2}{2\Dt}\right].
\end{equation}
The likelihood factors are \emph{unary}: because the forward noise is independent across
coordinates, each observation touches one latent variable. They therefore reweight node
potentials and add no edges, and the posterior factor graph is the prior's chain
(Figure~\ref{fig:factorgraph}). A chain is a tree, so sum-product is exact
\citep{pearl1988,mezard2009}: with
\begin{align}
\label{eq:messages}
\lmsg_{i}(a_i) &\propto \int \mathrm{d}a_{i-1}\;
\kernel(a_i\mid a_{i-1})\,\like(x_{i-1}\mid a_{i-1})\,\lmsg_{i-1}(a_{i-1}), \nonumber\\[-2pt]
\rmsg_{i}(a_i) &\propto \int \mathrm{d}a_{i+1}\;
\kernel(a_{i+1}\mid a_i)\,\like(x_{i+1}\mid a_{i+1})\,\rmsg_{i+1}(a_{i+1}),
\end{align}
initialised by $\lmsg_1 = \mu$ and $\rmsg_{\nsites}\equiv 1$ --- without which the initial law
would enter no marginal --- the single-site marginal is
$P(a_i\mid x,t) \propto \lmsg_i(a_i)\like(x_i\mid a_i)\rmsg_i(a_i)$
and the pairwise marginal on edge $(i-1,i)$ is
$\propto \lmsg_{i-1}(a_{i-1})\,\like(x_{i-1}\mid a_{i-1})\,\kernel(a_i\mid a_{i-1})\,
\like(x_i\mid a_i)\,\rmsg_i(a_i)$. In
\eqref{eq:messages} the local likelihood is absorbed \emph{before} propagation, so
$\like(x_i\mid a_i)$ appears exactly once in each marginal; this convention is fixed
throughout.

\begin{remark}[What ``exact'' means here]
\label{rem:exact}
Three statements must be kept apart. (i) Sum-product on the posterior \eqref{eq:posterior} is
exact at the level of functional messages, as a consequence of the graph being a tree. (ii)
Messages are functions on $\Reals$, so an implementation must represent them; we use a
truncated uniform grid with composite trapezoidal weights, which defines a finite-state model
for which the recursion is exact up to floating-point arithmetic. (iii) That finite-state
model approximates the continuous one, with an error that must be measured rather than
assumed. We write ``exact tree inference'' for (i) and ``exact for the discretised model''
for (ii), and never abbreviate either to ``exact''.
\end{remark}

\section{Numerical control}
\label{sec:numerics}

Two error sources are separated by construction. \emph{Truncation}: probability mass outside
the grid $[-A,A]$ is discarded, monitored by the mass in the boundary cells. \textsc{[pending]} The diagnostic is computed
inside the recursion but not persisted, so no aggregate figure is quoted. \emph{Quadrature}: the trapezoidal rule is
second order in the spacing for integrands with a bounded second derivative. For the Gaussian
kernel the integrand is smooth and convergence is fast; \textbf{for the Laplace kernel it is
not}, since $\innov(e)\propto e^{-|e|/b}$ has a discontinuous derivative at the origin, and we
therefore make no claim of spectral accuracy for that family.

Against the closed-form Gaussian posterior mean, the discretised recursion agrees to
$9.2\times10^{-15}$ relative at $\ngrid=401$, $A=8$. That number measures the discretisation
of the Gaussian model only. It is not comparable with the independent Monte Carlo check
below, which measures something else: on the Laplace chain, where no closed form exists,
an independent importance-sampling estimate agrees with the discretised recursion: the
evidence to within five Monte Carlo standard errors at $10^6$ samples, and the posterior mean
to $10^{-2}$ relative at $2\times10^6$ (\codefile{tests/test\_em\_bp.py}). These are assertions
in the test suite rather than recorded measurements, and their tolerances are set by the Monte
Carlo error rather than by the grid. Both are reported because they validate different things; neither is the accuracy of
the other.

\section{Gaussian closure and the LMMSE estimator}
\label{sec:closure}

A standard approximation propagates only the first two moments of each message. Under the
transition $a_i = \corr a_{i-1} + \varepsilon_i$, a message with mean $m$ and variance $v$ transforms as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:closure}
\text{forward:}\quad m \mapsto \corr m,\;\; v \mapsto \corr^2 v + \ivar ;
\qquad
\text{backward:}\quad m \mapsto m/\corr,\;\; v \mapsto (v+\ivar)/\corr^2 ,
\end{equation}
the backward map being the forward one inverted because that recursion integrates over the
\emph{output} variable (it requires $\corr\neq0$),
and a Gaussian message times a Gaussian likelihood is Gaussian. The propagation therefore
uses only the mean and variance of $\innov$ and discards everything else about its shape.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:lmmse}
For the chain \eqref{eq:prior} with linear transitions, Gaussian unary likelihoods and
$\E[a]=0$, moment-projected sum-product returns the LMMSE estimator of the covariance-matched
Gaussian model,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lmmse}
\lmmse
= \Cov(a,x)\,\Cov(x)^{-1} x
= e^{-t}\Sigma_0\left(e^{-2t}\Sigma_0 + \Dt I\right)^{-1} x ,
\end{equation}
for every innovation law with mean zero and variance $\ivar$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Both maps in \eqref{eq:closure} depend on $\innov$ only through its mean and variance, so the
recursion returns the same function of $x$ for every innovation law with moments $(0,\ivar)$
--- in particular the same as on the covariance-matched \emph{Gaussian} chain. On that chain
every message is exactly Gaussian, so the moment projection is the identity and the recursion
is exact; exact inference returns the posterior mean, which for jointly Gaussian zero-mean
$(a,x)$ coincides with the LMMSE estimator by Proposition~\ref{prop:orthogonality} restricted
to linear estimators.
\end{proof}
\noindent
On the Laplace chain the information-form recursion is verified against the closed-form
Gaussian posterior mean to $10^{-12}$ (\codefile{tests/test\_gaussian\_bp\_equivalence.py}).
\textsc{[pending]} The corresponding check across the full set of innovation families is not
currently recorded as an output. The zero-mean
hypothesis is not cosmetic: with a prior mean $\E[a]=1.3$ the estimator \eqref{eq:lmmse}
departs from the true LMMSE by $0.65$ in maximum absolute value, since the general form
carries $\E[a] + \Cov(a,x)\Cov(x)^{-1}(x-\E[x])$.

\begin{remark}
Proposition~\ref{prop:lmmse} is a statement about \emph{this} closure --- single-Gaussian
moment projection with linear transitions --- and not about Gaussian approximations in
general. Within its scope it has a consequence worth stating: what one might call the
message-representation error and the model error coincide, because projecting the messages
and replacing the model by its covariance-matched Gaussian give the same estimator. The two
separate again for richer message families.
\end{remark}

The gap between \eqref{eq:lmmse} and exact inference locates where non-Gaussianity matters.
It is largest at \emph{small} $t$ and decays monotonically: on a Laplace chain at
$\corr=0.85$, the relative deviation of the closure's score from the exact one falls from
$0.198$ at $t=0.08$ to $9.9\times10^{-5}$ at $t=2.4$. Large $t$ Gaussianises the posterior, so
the shape of $\innov$ survives only where the likelihood is weak.

\section{Estimating the transition kernel}
\label{sec:em}

We now treat $\kernel$ as unknown and observe only noised sequences $x^\mu$, $\mu=1,\dots,\nseq$,
possibly at several noise levels. The initial law $\mu(a_1)$ is held fixed (see
\S\ref{sec:limits}). Write $\params$ for the kernel parameters and
$\Lik(\params)=\sum_\mu \log P_{t,\params}(x^\mu)$.

\paragraph{Parametrisation.} We use a mixture-innovation kernel
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kernel}
\kernel_\params(a'\mid a) \;=\; \sum_{c=1}^{C} \pi_c \,
\mathcal{N}\!\left(a' - \corr a;\; \nu_c,\, s_c^2\right),
\end{equation}
which keeps the linear autoregression and leaves the innovation law essentially free. At
$C=4$ this has $\corr$, four weights $\pi_c$, four locations $\nu_c$ and four scales $s_c^2$:
thirteen numbers subject to $\sum_c\pi_c=1$, hence \textbf{twelve free parameters}. It is
never given the density it is asked to recover.

\paragraph{Exact expectation step.} Many latent-variable models require an approximate or
variational E-step; this one does not, because the posterior is a tree
(\S\ref{sec:bp}) and sum-product returns exact single-site \emph{and pairwise} marginals. The
complete-data log-likelihood is a sum over edges, so the \emph{$\params$-dependent part} of the
auxiliary function $\Qfun(\params\mid\params^{(r)})$ --- it also contains a site-one term and a
likelihood term, both $\params$-free and therefore additive constants --- depends on the data
only through
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:xi}
\Xis_{kl} \;=\; \sum_{\mu=1}^{\nseq}\sum_{i=2}^{\nsites}
P\!\left(a_{i-1}=u_k,\; a_i=u_l \;\middle|\; x^\mu\right),
\end{equation}
the expected transition mass between grid points $u_k$ and $u_l$ --- the continuum analogue of
the expected transition counts of Baum--Welch \citep{baum1970}. Sufficiency of
\eqref{eq:xi} in that sense requires a \emph{homogeneous} kernel shared across sites and
sequences, a fixed grid, an initial law excluded from $\params$, and $\params$-free likelihood
factors --- the last being what licenses observations at several noise levels to add into one
$\Xis$. It fails if any of these is relaxed.
Given those, the maximisation step never revisits the data and costs $O(P\ngrid^2)$ for $P$
parameters, independently of $\nseq$; observations at different noise levels add into one
$\Xis$. Forming $\Xis$ costs $O(\nseq\nsites\ngrid^2)$ per iteration and $O(\ngrid^2)$
storage, so ``one matrix'' is a statement about sufficiency, not about inference being cheap.

\paragraph{Gradient without differentiating the recursion.} Fisher's identity
\citep{fisher1925,cappe2005} gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fisher}
\nabla_\params \Lik(\params)
= \sum_{\mu}\E_\params\!\left[\nabla_\params \log P_\params(a,x^\mu)\,\middle|\,x^\mu\right]
= \big\langle \Xis(\params),\; \nabla_\params \log \kernel_\params \big\rangle ,
\end{equation}
the exact gradient of the \emph{marginal} log-likelihood from one sum-product pass. Both
sides are summed over the $\nseq$ observed sequences, matching the definition of $\Xis$. Sum-product
is not a computation graph to backpropagate through; it supplies the conditional expectation.
We verify \eqref{eq:fisher} against finite differences of the exact evidence to $\sim10^{-9}$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:fisher} is not EM. It licenses \emph{gradient ascent} on $\Lik$, which is
the procedure described in the guidance we were given, whereas EM maximises
$\Qfun$ at each step. Both are implemented and they do not dominate one another: on the
smooth Gaussian kernel gradient ascent reaches EM's optimum to $2\times10^{-9}$ nats while
requiring a tuned step size, and on the Laplace kernel it attains a higher likelihood than
EM, because there the exact maximiser of $\Qfun$ is a lattice artefact of the discretisation.
Results below use EM. For the mixture kernel \eqref{eq:kernel} the maximisation step is
itself iterative --- the complete-data problem carries a second latent variable, the mixture
label, and we run four inner conditional-maximisation sweeps --- so it is \emph{generalised}
EM: each step increases $\Qfun$ without exactly maximising it, which preserves monotone ascent
but not the exact-maximiser property.

\paragraph{Identifiability.} Two claims must be separated. Gaussian convolution at finite $t$
is injective on distributions --- the characteristic function acquires a factor
$e^{-\Dt\|\xi\|^2/2}$, which has no zeros, and $e^{-t}>0$ --- so $\Pt$ determines $\Pz$.
\emph{Kernel} identifiability does not follow: it additionally requires the initial law to be
fixed or separately identifiable, the transition family to be identifiable as a parametric family, and the mixture
\eqref{eq:kernel} to be treated modulo label permutation. Two further conditions are easy to
overlook. Finite-mixture identifiability holds only on the interior of the parameter set ---
all $\pi_c>0$ and the pairs $(\nu_c,s_c)$ distinct --- which is exactly where an
over-specified $C$ need not stay; and $\kernel$ is pinned down only on the support of $\mu$,
so a full-support initial law is required. We assume these rather than prove them. We also
note that the fitted innovation is not constrained to have zero mean: imposing
$\sum_c\pi_c\nu_c=0$ by reprojection is not a maximisation step and broke monotone ascent in
testing, so the condition is monitored as a diagnostic (it lands at the $10^{-3}$ level) rather
than enforced. Proposition~\ref{prop:lmmse} therefore applies to the true kernel, not
automatically to the estimated one.

What noising does destroy is \emph{information}, and unevenly. Measured per sequence from
exact single-chain passes, the Fisher information for the innovation scale falls by a factor
$142$ between $t=0.05$ and $t=1.6$, while that for the correlation falls by $26$. The
higher-order structure of $\innov$ is thus far more fragile under the channel than its
second-order structure --- a fact we return to in \S\ref{sec:generation}.

Table~\ref{tab:pointwise} shows a ratio between $9$ and $14$ across seven doublings of the
data, with no trend in the budget. The network column takes, at each noise level, the better
of the two parametrisations, as the protocol above states; reporting the noise-predicting
parametrisation alone --- as an earlier version of this table did --- would overstate the
network's deficit by $15$ to $30\%$, since predicting the clean signal is the better choice at
$t\in\{0.8,1.6\}$ at every budget. The comparison at equal budget is the meaningful one. We note that the network at
$\nseq=4096$ did not reach the error the estimator attains at $\nseq=32$; \textbf{we did not
observe a crossing}, so we do not quantify a data-equivalence factor, and the extrapolation
that would be required to do so spans several decades.

\paragraph{Controls.} Capacity is not the explanation: over $24$ configurations from $3.2$k
to $297$k parameters the best network reaches $0.208$ against the estimator's $0.022$ at
$\nseq=1024$, and error degrades for the largest models. The baseline is competent: trained on a Gaussian chain where the optimal denoiser is linear
and closed-form, with $3000$ sequences and $12000$ steps, it passes the competence threshold
asserted in \codefile{tests/test\_em\_bp.py}. The achieved value is not persisted, so it is
not quoted.

\paragraph{A locality-respecting baseline.} A fully connected network carries no sequential
prior. Against a weight-shared window predictor --- a one-dimensional convolution --- the
margin narrows substantially, from roughly $10$ to between $2$ and $4$ over
$\nseq\in\{128,512,2048\}$. Between $58\%$ and $73\%$ of the fully connected network's deficit was therefore its
architecture rather than the estimator, depending on the budget. The margin against the convolution
increases over the three budgets we ran --- $2.31\pm0.39$, $3.36\pm0.30$, $4.16\pm0.24$ as
ratios of seed-averaged errors, or $2.72$, $3.78$, $5.04$ if per-noise-level ratios are formed
first. Three points do not establish a trend, and the aggregation choice moves the value, so we
report the range. \textbf{These particular
numbers used a receptive field selected on the evaluation set} and are exploratory pending a
rerun with a validation split; the bias favours the baseline, so the margin quoted is if
anything conservative.

\section{Generation}
\label{sec:generation}

Denoising accuracy is not what a generative model is judged on. Errors enter the reverse
integration at every step and accumulate unevenly across the schedule, so a pointwise ranking
need not survive. We therefore integrate the reverse process
$\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}\tau = x + 2\score(x,\tau) + \sqrt{2}\,\gwn$ under each estimator (the noise $\gwn$ is standardised throughout, so the $\sqrt{2}$ is explicit on both the forward and the reverse process), with common
random numbers across estimators.

\paragraph{Comparing like with like.} Integration must stop at $t_{\min}>0$, since
$\score$ diverges as $\Dt\to0$. What each estimator produces is therefore a sample of
$P_{t_{\min}}$, not of $\Pz$, and comparing it against clean data measures the stopping point
rather than the score: independent Gaussian noise of variance $v$ on an innovation of variance
$\ivar$ multiplies the excess kurtosis by $(\ivar/(\ivar+v))^2$. We instead draw a reference
from $\Pz$ and noise it forward to the same $t_{\min}$, so both sides are samples of the same
law and the target moments follow in closed form. Run with the \emph{true} kernel, the sampler
reproduces that target to within $1.4\%$ across budgets --- medians $1.897$, $1.883$, $1.914$,
$1.908$ at $\nseq=32,128,512,2048$ against a closed-form $1.9098$ --- which validates the
integrator and the statistics before any estimator is compared. The spread is sampling noise
and is not systematic in $\nseq$.

Table~\ref{tab:generation} is the paper's main empirical finding, and it is a dissociation.
The structured estimator is an order of magnitude more accurate pointwise and three times
better on second-order structure, and yet the convolutional network reproduces the
\emph{innovation law} more faithfully, by both kurtosis and divergence. The fully connected
network shows the pattern most sharply: over the same range its denoising error improves
twelvefold while its generated kurtosis degrades from $0.850$ to $0.206$. It fits the bulk of
the posterior and loses the tail.

\paragraph{The dissociation generalises.} It is not a property of the Laplace chain, of the
chain length, or of the quadrature. Across the four non-Gaussian families of
\S\ref{sec:setting} at matched covariance the convolutional network is closer to the target
innovation kurtosis than the estimator at $C=4$ in every case (Laplace $1.273$ against
$0.812$, target $1.921$; Student $1.478$ against $0.989$, target $2.940$; and likewise for the
two negatively-kurtotic families). It is unchanged at $\nsites\in\{32,64,128\}$, and
\textbf{identical to three decimals} under grid refinement $\ngrid\in\{401,801,1601\}$, which
rules out quadrature as the cause.

\paragraph{The cause is capacity, not information.}
Two explanations were available. Both the marginal likelihood the estimator maximises and the
squared-error denoising loss are dominated by the bulk of the distribution, so the tail ---
little mass, high leverage on shape statistics --- is weakly constrained by either; and
separately, the channel destroys innovation-shape information $142$-fold against $26$-fold for
the correlation (\S\ref{sec:em}), so the data itself may not carry it. The first is a limit of
the model class and is fixable; the second is a limit of the observation model and is not.

Varying $C$ separates them (Table~\ref{tab:components}). The generated kurtosis climbs
monotonically and is still climbing at $C=16$, so the deficit at $C=4$ is \textbf{capacity}.
The estimator overtakes the network between $C=4$ and $C=8$ and is ahead of it by $C=16$.

\section{Limitations and scope}
\label{sec:limits}

The model assumes exact first-order Markov structure; under a non-Markov prior the estimator
is misspecified in a way more data does not repair. The transition family
\eqref{eq:kernel} may additionally be misspecified, and \S\ref{sec:generation} suggests it is,
for the tails. The initial law is fixed rather than estimated. Sum-product is exact on the
posterior chain but the implementation is a discretisation whose error, for the Laplace
family, is second order rather than spectral.

Inference costs $O(\nsites\ngrid^2)$ per sequence per evaluation against a few matrix products
for a network, measured here at two to three hundred times slower; since reverse diffusion
calls the denoiser at every step, this is the practical weak point. Training is also slower,
and the estimator's cost grows linearly in $\nseq$ while the network's is fixed by its step
count, so the advantage is in data and not in wall-clock. The low parameter count is not free:
it is bought with a structural assumption, and the advantage narrows as the parametric
dimension grows --- on a discrete-alphabet variant it falls from $15$ to $4.4$ as the
estimated dimension goes from $6$ to $132$ parameters.

Error bars over training seeds measure optimisation variance at fixed data, since the seeds
vary the initialisation and not the sample. The family, length and grid sweeps use a single
mixture size, so the interaction between $C$ and pointwise accuracy is not yet measured, and
the claim that a richer kernel improves generation is established for the generated
distribution only. Comparisons are against the two architectures described and do not license
a claim about neural denoisers in general. The discrete-alphabet figure quoted above is
aggregation-sensitive: the $15$ is robust, the $4.4$ is not, and the two endpoints are not
computed the same way.

\section{Conclusion}

When a sequential distribution is known to have low-dimensional Markov structure, learning a
diffusion score reduces to latent-variable inference and estimation of a compact transition
model. Sum-product on the noised chain supplies an exact non-Gaussian benchmark, and
estimating the kernel from noised data alone gives a denoiser that is substantially more
accurate at small sample sizes than trained networks, at the cost of stronger assumptions and
much more expensive inference. The comparison after reverse diffusion does not reproduce that ranking at a fixed small
parametrisation, and the reason turns out to be the capacity of the innovation model rather
than the information available in noised data. We regard the methodological point as the more
durable one: an estimator can be an order of magnitude better at the task it was fitted for
and still generate worse samples, so a structured method must be judged on the generated
distribution and not on denoising error alone.
